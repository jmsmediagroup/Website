Your manager sends a message that says "Can we chat tomorrow?" with no further detail. By the time you finish reading it, your chest is tight, your face is hot, and you have already drafted your resignation in your head.

Tomorrow's chat turns out to be about holiday dates.

If that pattern is familiar, the term you may be looking for is rejection sensitive dysphoria. It is not a formal diagnosis and you will not find it in a diagnostic manual, but it is widely used in ADHD communities because it names something a great many of us recognise on first hearing.

## Key takeaways

- RSD is intense emotional pain in response to real or perceived rejection, criticism or failure.
- The reaction arrives fast, feels physical, and is wildly out of proportion to the trigger.
- It often hides behind people-pleasing, perfectionism, or avoiding anything you might fail at.
- The feeling cannot be argued away in the moment, but the response to it can be delayed.
- It is common in ADHD and overlaps with anxiety, so persistent distress is worth taking to a professional.

## What it feels like

The defining feature is speed. There is no gradual build. One moment you are fine, the next you are flooded, and the flood is physical: heat in the face, a drop in the stomach, sometimes tears that arrive before you have decided anything.

The second feature is scale. A minor piece of feedback lands with the weight of a verdict on your whole person. The distance between "this paragraph could be tighter" and "you are fundamentally not good enough" collapses to nothing.

The third is that it does not need actual rejection. Ambiguity is enough. A delayed reply, a flat tone, someone leaving a meeting early, a friend who seems slightly quieter than usual. The brain fills the gap with the worst available interpretation and then responds to that interpretation as fact.

People describe it as a burn, or a punch, or the floor going. Those are not exaggerations. The response is out of proportion to the trigger, but the pain itself is real.

## How it hides

RSD does not always look like an emotional reaction from outside. Often it looks like a personality.

People-pleasing is one of the most common disguises. If disapproval hurts that much, preventing it becomes a full-time job: saying yes to everything, never disagreeing, reading every room for signs of displeasure, apologising pre-emptively.

Perfectionism is another. Work gets polished far past the point of usefulness because handing it in means exposing it to judgement. Or it never gets handed in at all.

Avoidance is the quietest one. Not applying for the job. Not sending the message. Not starting the project, the relationship, the hobby, because trying means the possibility of failing, and failing means the burn. From outside this reads as a lack of ambition. From inside it is a reasonable calculation by someone who knows exactly how much it will hurt.

And sometimes it looks like anger. The pain turns outward, fast, at whoever triggered it. That tends to damage the relationships the sensitivity was trying to protect, which then feeds more fear of rejection.

> From outside, RSD often looks like a personality: the people-pleaser, the perfectionist, the person who never quite tries.

## Why ADHD and RSD go together

The honest answer is that the mechanism is not fully understood, and I am not going to pretend otherwise. What is well described is that emotional dysregulation, difficulty managing the intensity of emotions, is extremely common in adults with ADHD, even though it is not part of the formal diagnostic criteria. RSD reads like a specific, sharp form of that.

There is also a history argument that many people find persuasive from their own lives. If you grew up with undiagnosed ADHD, you probably received a great deal of correction. Forgotten homework, lost belongings, "you're not applying yourself", "why can't you just". Years of that can build a very sensitive alarm around criticism, because criticism was frequent and often aimed at things you could not control.

Autistic adults describe similar patterns, often tangled up with masking. When you have spent years monitoring other people's faces for signs that your performance is slipping, any sign of disapproval carries extra charge.

## What helps

The feeling itself does not respond well to logic in the moment. Telling yourself it is an overreaction while you are mid-flood is about as effective as telling yourself a burn does not hurt. What you can change is what you do while the feeling is running.

Name it, specifically. "This is RSD" is more useful than "I'm upset", because it labels the reaction as a known pattern rather than accurate information about the situation. It does not stop the pain. It does stop you treating the pain as evidence.

Put a delay between the feeling and any response. No replies, no decisions, no resignation emails for a fixed period. An hour is often enough. The flood usually passes faster than it feels like it will, and almost every regrettable action happens inside it.

Check the story against the facts once the intensity has dropped. Write down what actually happened, in neutral language, and then write down the story your brain built on top of it. "Manager asked to chat tomorrow" and "I am about to be fired" are very different sentences, and seeing them side by side is often enough.

Ask, if it is safe to. "Is everything okay between us?" or "Was there something specific about this you'd like changed?" feels terrifying and usually produces a mundane answer. Many people with RSD have spent years avoiding questions that would have resolved the uncertainty in a sentence.

Choose your exposures. Some environments run on constant feedback, public evaluation and ambiguous signals. If you have a choice, it is reasonable to factor RSD into it, the same way you would factor in any other known sensitivity.

And tell one or two trusted people. Partners and close friends who know about RSD can be more explicit: "I'm not annoyed, just tired" removes an enormous amount of guesswork.

## When to get help

If the reactions are frequent, severe, or are pushing you away from work and relationships you care about, it is worth raising with a professional. RSD overlaps heavily with anxiety and depression, and those deserve proper assessment rather than self-management alone. Some people with ADHD find that treatment changes their emotional reactivity, and that is a conversation to have with a prescriber.

If criticism or rejection ever leaves you thinking about harming yourself, please contact a crisis line or emergency services where you are.

## Common questions

### Is rejection sensitive dysphoria a real diagnosis?

No. It is not a formal diagnosis and does not appear in diagnostic manuals. It is a widely used description, particularly in ADHD communities, for an intense emotional response to perceived rejection or criticism. The experience is real even though the label is informal.

### Is RSD only an ADHD thing?

It is most often discussed in relation to ADHD, but similar patterns are described by autistic people and by people with anxiety and other conditions. Sensitivity to rejection is a human trait. What people with RSD describe is the speed and intensity of the response, which goes well beyond the ordinary.

### How is RSD different from just being sensitive?

Mainly in speed, scale and physicality. People with RSD describe an almost instant, overwhelming reaction to minor or ambiguous signals, felt in the body, that can take hours to settle. Ordinary sensitivity tends to be proportionate and to fade quickly.

### Can RSD be treated?

Some people find that ADHD treatment reduces their emotional reactivity, and therapy can help with the patterns built around it, such as avoidance and people-pleasing. Those are conversations for a doctor or therapist. The practical strategies here help with managing reactions, not with treating anything.

### What can I do in the moment?

Label it as RSD, then delay any response for at least an hour. The feeling will be loud, but most of the damage comes from acting inside it. [The ADHD Overthinking Reset](/books/adhd-overthinking-reset/) has a section on RSD spirals specifically.
