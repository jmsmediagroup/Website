I have changed career on a fortnight's reflection and lost most of an evening to choosing between two almost identical kettles.

That ratio is the thing worth explaining, because it looks like inconsistency and it is not. The small decisions are harder, and there is a reason for it.

## Key takeaways

- Small decisions are often harder than large ones, because nothing forces them to close.
- Without a clear criterion, every option looks equally defensible and comparison never ends.
- Paralysis is frequently emotional avoidance rather than genuine uncertainty.
- Setting a stopping rule in advance works better than trying to decide faster.
- Most small decisions are reversible, and treating them as reversible removes most of the weight.

## Why small decisions are harder

Big decisions arrive with structure. They have deadlines, consequences that focus the mind, and usually other people involved who need an answer. The structure forces a conclusion.

Small decisions have none of that. Nobody needs your kettle decision by Thursday. There is no penalty for taking another hour. So the process has no natural end, and without an end it can continue indefinitely.

Worse, the options in small decisions tend to be close together. Two kettles that do the same thing differ in ways that are real but tiny, which means the comparison can always be refined further. There is always another review, another specification, another dimension you have not weighed. The closer the options, the longer you can legitimately keep comparing, which is the opposite of how it should work.

Then there is the missing criterion. For a big decision you usually know what matters. For small ones you often do not, so you end up trying to optimise across everything at once: price, quality, reviews, appearance, longevity, delivery time, ethics of the manufacturer. No option wins on all of them, so no option ever clearly wins.

> Two near-identical options cannot be meaningfully separated, which is exactly why you can keep trying forever.

## When it is avoidance wearing a disguise

Sometimes the paralysis is not about the decision at all.

If choosing means committing, and committing means possibly being wrong, then not choosing keeps every outcome theoretically still available. Deliberating feels productive while actually functioning as a way of staying still.

The signs are fairly reliable once you know them. Researching long past the point where new information is changing anything. Reaching a conclusion and then reopening it. Feeling relief when something prevents the decision from having to be made. Asking several people and discounting whichever answer you did not want.

Rejection sensitivity sharpens this considerably. If being wrong feels catastrophic rather than inconvenient, deferring the decision is doing emotional work, and no amount of additional information will resolve something that is not an information problem.

## What actually helps

Decide how you will decide, before you start. One criterion, chosen in advance. Cheapest that meets a basic standard. Best reviewed under a price. The one available soonest. Having a rule converts an open comparison into a lookup, and a lookup terminates.

Put a time limit on it and treat the limit as binding. Ten minutes for anything under a certain value, and when the timer goes you take whatever is currently ahead. The quality of a decision made in ten minutes versus ninety is usually indistinguishable at this scale, and the eighty minutes are gone either way.

Cut the list to three. Most paralysis is caused by too many options, and the difficulty rises fast with the number. Take the first three that meet your criterion, discard everything else without examining it, and choose among those.

Check reversibility explicitly. Most small decisions are easily undone: returns, cancellations, trying again next week. Asking what happens if this is wrong usually produces an answer like I return it, which deflates most of the pressure. Keep the careful deliberation for decisions that are genuinely hard to reverse, which are rarer than they feel.

Automate repeating decisions permanently. Anything you decide more than once a month deserves a default: same lunch, same brand, same supermarket slot. The decision is made once and never again. This is where the largest savings are, because recurring decisions are where most of the time actually goes.

And accept good enough as a target rather than a compromise. The pursuit of the best option is what most small decisions founder on, and the gap between the best choice and a decent one at this scale is almost always smaller than the cost of finding it.

## If you are stuck right now

Pick the one you were leaning toward in the first ten seconds.

That instinct is usually as good as anything that follows, and in my experience the extended deliberation after it rarely changes the answer. It mostly adds justification to a decision already made, or exhausts you into choosing nothing at all, which is the worst available outcome because you have paid the full cost and received none of the benefit.

If you genuinely have no instinct, the options are close enough that it does not matter. Take either. The symmetry that makes the choice hard is the same symmetry that makes it low stakes.

## Common questions

### Why can I make big decisions easily but not small ones?

Big decisions come with deadlines, consequences and other people, all of which force closure. Small ones have no structure, close options, and no obvious criterion, so the comparison can continue indefinitely. The size of the decision and the difficulty of making it are not well correlated.

### Is decision paralysis an ADHD thing?

Difficulty with decision-making is commonly reported by ADHD adults and relates to executive function: holding options in mind, weighing future outcomes, and inhibiting further deliberation. It is not exclusive to ADHD, and it is also strongly associated with anxiety, which frequently coexists.

### How do I stop researching?

Decide the stopping condition before you begin, because you will not be able to judge it reliably once you are inside the process. Three options, ten minutes, one criterion. The point is that the limit is set by a version of you who is not currently in the loop.

### Does this get worse when I am tired?

Usually, and noticeably. Decision-making degrades with fatigue, and inhibition degrades with it, so you are both less able to choose and less able to stop deliberating. Postponing non-urgent decisions when you are depleted is a reasonable strategy rather than an avoidance.

### What is the single most useful habit?

Defaults for anything recurring. Deciding once and never again removes the largest share of decisions from your week, and it is where the biggest return is. [The ADHD Decision Reset](https://www.amazon.com/dp/B0GDMWFDZ7) covers building those defaults and the stopping rules in more depth.
