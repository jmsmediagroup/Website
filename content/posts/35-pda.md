There is a particular experience that ordinary descriptions of procrastination do not cover. Someone asks you to do a small thing you were about to do anyway, and something in you locks. Not reluctance. Not laziness. A physical refusal that arrives before any thought does, attached to a task you genuinely wanted to complete thirty seconds earlier.

If you have tried to explain that and been told you are being difficult, the term you may be looking for is PDA.

## Key takeaways

- PDA describes extreme avoidance of everyday demands, understood as anxiety-driven rather than wilful.
- It is not a formal diagnosis in the main diagnostic manuals.
- It is usually described as a profile within autism, and the concept is used far more in the UK than elsewhere.
- Demands include ones you set yourself, which is what distinguishes it from ordinary defiance.
- Most of what helps involves reducing the demand quality of a request rather than enforcing it.

## What it describes

PDA stands for pathological demand avoidance. Many people dislike the word pathological and use pervasive drive for autonomy instead, and some clinicians prefer extreme demand avoidance. The terminology is genuinely unsettled, and which phrase someone uses often signals how they think about it.

What it points at is an avoidance of demands that goes well beyond preference, is driven by anxiety, and applies to demands of every kind.

That last part is the distinctive bit. The avoidance is not limited to things other people want. It attaches to things you want too. People describe being unable to start a hobby they were looking forward to, unable to eat when hungry because eating has become a demand, unable to reply to a message from someone they like. A demand you placed on yourself can trigger it as completely as an instruction from a manager.

It also responds to framing more than content. The same task can be impossible phrased as an instruction and manageable phrased as a choice, a game, or an idea you arrived at yourself. From outside this looks like inconsistency or manipulation. From inside it is the difference between a demand and a non-demand.

## The honest status of the idea

I want to be straight about this, because a lot of writing on PDA is not.

PDA is not a formal diagnosis. It does not appear as a separate category in the main diagnostic manuals, and in most places you cannot be diagnosed with it. Where clinicians use it, they generally diagnose autism and describe a PDA profile within that.

It is also geographically uneven. The concept originated in the UK and is used widely there, much less in the United States and elsewhere, which means your clinician's familiarity with it depends heavily on where you are.

And the evidence base is thin, particularly for adults. Most of what exists concerns children. Reviews have noted how little is available on how it presents or is managed in adulthood, which is worth knowing if you are an adult trying to find anything useful.

None of that makes the experience less real. Plenty of useful descriptions precede their formal recognition, and some never get it. It does mean you should be careful of anyone selling certainty about it, and that a clinician being unfamiliar with the term is not necessarily a judgement about you.

> The experience is real whether or not the label is official. Both of those things can be true, and it helps to hold them at once.

## How it differs from things it gets confused with

| | What is going on |
| --- | --- |
| PDA | The demand itself triggers anxiety, including demands you set yourself |
| Ordinary procrastination | The task is unappealing or aversive; a different task is often chosen instead |
| ADHD task paralysis | Wanting to start and being unable to initiate, with no particular objection to being asked |
| Defiance | Aimed at a person or authority, and usually responsive to what they do next |
| Burnout | Capacity has run out; most things are hard, not specifically demanded things |

[ADHD task paralysis](/blog/adhd-task-paralysis/) is the one most often confused with it, and the two can look identical from outside. The distinguishing question is usually whether being asked makes it worse. With task paralysis, how the task arrived is mostly irrelevant. With demand avoidance, the asking is the problem.

## What tends to help

Almost everything useful reduces how much a thing feels like a demand.

Declarative language rather than instructions. "The bins go out tonight" lands differently from "can you put the bins out". The information is identical; one is a demand and one is a statement.

Choice, genuinely offered. Two acceptable options are often workable where one instruction is not, because choosing restores some autonomy.

Indirectness. Writing instead of speaking, leaving a note, letting something be discovered rather than announced.

Lowering the stakes out loud. Saying the thing can be half done, or abandoned, or done tomorrow, removes the pressure that is generating the avoidance.

Removing yourself as the source where you can. Automating, scheduling, or letting a timer or an app make the request means it is not coming from a person, which for many people takes most of the charge out of it.

And self-directed demands need the same treatment. People describe getting further by framing things as experiments, or as optional, or by deliberately not writing a list, because a list becomes a set of demands the moment it exists.

If you are supporting someone, the hardest and most useful adjustment is usually accepting that pushing harder makes it worse rather than better. Escalation is the one approach reliably reported to backfire.

## Common questions

### What does PDA look like in adults?

Mostly like being unable to do things you want to do, once they have become a demand. Adults describe freezing when asked to do something they were about to do anyway, being unable to start a hobby they were looking forward to, putting off eating when hungry, and leaving messages from people they like unanswered. It applies to demands you set yourself as well as other people's, and the same task can feel impossible as an instruction and fine as a choice.

### Is PDA a real diagnosis?

Not a formal one. PDA does not appear as a separate category in the main diagnostic manuals, and in most places it cannot be diagnosed on its own. Clinicians who use the concept generally diagnose autism and describe a PDA profile within it. The experience it describes is widely reported regardless.

### What is the difference between PDA and ordinary procrastination?

Procrastination usually attaches to tasks that are unappealing, and people often do something else instead. With demand avoidance the trigger is the demand itself, including demands you set yourself, and it applies to things you actively want to do. Being asked tends to make it worse rather than better.

### Why do some people say "pervasive drive for autonomy"?

Because many find "pathological" stigmatising, particularly when applied to a response that makes sense as anxiety about loss of control. Some clinicians use "extreme demand avoidance" for similar reasons. The terminology is genuinely unsettled.

### Is PDA recognised outside the UK?

Much less so. The concept originated in the UK and is used widely there, while clinicians elsewhere, including in the United States, are often unfamiliar with it. If you raise it and get a blank response, that may reflect where you are rather than anything about your experience.

### What helps most?

Reducing the demand quality of requests: declarative language instead of instructions, real choices, indirect delivery, lowering the stakes, and letting something other than a person make the ask. Escalating pressure is the approach most consistently reported to make things worse.
