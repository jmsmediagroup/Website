You know the meeting is at ten. You know it takes twenty minutes to get there. At 9:52 you are still in the shower, and you could not tell anyone how it got to be 9:52.

People who are reliably on time tend to assume lateness means not caring. For a lot of ADHD adults it is closer to the opposite: you cared enough to think about the meeting all morning, and still had no felt sense of the morning passing.

The usual name for this is time blindness. It is a community term rather than a clinical one, and it describes the experience well.

## Key takeaways

- Time blindness is difficulty feeling time pass and estimating how long things take.
- ADHD time tends to split into "now" and "not now", with very little in between.
- It is a perception problem, so effort and caring do not fix it.
- The solution is to make time visible and external, rather than trying to feel it better.
- Estimates can be calibrated, but only by measuring, never by guessing harder.

## Now and not now

The most useful description I have come across is that ADHD time has two settings. There is now, which is vivid and urgent. And there is not now, which covers everything else, from an hour away to next year, and all of it feels roughly the same distance off.

That explains a lot of otherwise baffling behaviour. A deadline in three weeks does not register as more urgent than one in three months, because both are not now. Then it becomes now, suddenly, and everything happens in a panic.

It also explains the shower at 9:52. Ten o'clock was not now while you were getting ready. It was a fact you knew, not a pressure you felt. The pressure only arrives when the time has already gone.

## Estimates that are always wrong

The second part is estimation, and it fails in a consistent direction.

Most people with ADHD underestimate how long things take, often by a lot. Not because of optimism exactly, but because the estimate is built from the core of the task and leaves out everything around it. Getting to the shop takes ten minutes, in the sense that the drive takes ten minutes. It does not include finding keys, finding the other shoe, going back for the bag, the queue at the junction.

Transitions are where most of the time goes and where the estimate has nothing. Stopping one thing, getting up, moving, starting another. Each feels instant and each takes several minutes.

And hyperfocus breaks estimation completely. An hour of absorbed work and five minutes of absorbed work can feel identical from the inside.

> You cannot feel your way to better time sense. You have to move time outside your head, where you can see it.

## Waiting mode

A related pattern that many people only recognise once it is named: waiting mode.

You have an appointment at three. It is ten in the morning. And the whole day is now gone, because you cannot settle into anything. Starting a task feels risky, since you might lose track of time and miss the appointment, so you hover, half-doing small things, until three o'clock finally arrives.

It is time blindness turned into anxiety. You know you cannot trust your sense of time, so your brain refuses to let you do anything absorbing. The result is a day spent producing nothing while technically free.

Alarms help here more than anything else, specifically because they let your brain stop guarding the time.

## What actually helps

Everything that works follows the same principle: take time out of your head and put it somewhere you can see or hear it.

Visible clocks, everywhere. In every room you use, including the bathroom. Many people find analogue clocks easier than digital, because the shape of time remaining is visible rather than calculated. A visual timer that shows a shrinking coloured segment is the same idea in a more focused form.

Alarms for leaving, not for the event. An alarm at ten for a ten o'clock meeting is useless. Work backwards: meeting at ten, twenty minutes to get there, ten minutes to get out of the door, so the alarm is at 9:30, with a warning at 9:20. Set them as fixed habits rather than one-off reminders.

Pad every estimate by a fixed multiplier. Take your gut estimate and multiply it. Many people use 1.5 or 2. It feels absurd and it will still occasionally be wrong in the other direction, but it is much closer than the raw guess.

Calibrate with real measurement. For one week, time a handful of recurring tasks: getting ready, the commute, a load of washing, the weekly shop. Write the real numbers down. Real data beats intuition every time, and it is the only thing that actually improves your estimates.

Put everything in a calendar, including things that do not feel like appointments. Travel time. Getting-ready time. Lunch. If it is not in the calendar, it does not exist in your sense of time.

Use timers inside tasks. A timer every twenty or thirty minutes during absorbed work gives you a regular check-in with reality, and it works against hyperfocus quietly eating an afternoon.

And for waiting mode, set the alarm, trust the alarm, and deliberately start something absorbing. The alarm is doing the time-keeping job so your brain does not have to.

## Telling other people

Time blindness is very easy to read as disrespect, and it costs relationships. A short honest explanation tends to help more than repeated apologies, especially paired with visible effort: "I'm terrible with time, so I've set three alarms for this." People are generally more forgiving of a known difficulty being managed than of what looks like carelessness.

## Common questions

### Is time blindness an official ADHD symptom?

The term is informal and does not appear in diagnostic criteria. Difficulty with time management and with estimating time is very commonly reported by people with ADHD, and it relates to the executive functions involved in planning and organising.

### Why am I always late even when I try hard?

Because effort does not fix a perception problem. If you cannot feel time passing accurately, trying harder to feel it does not help. External tools such as visible clocks, leaving alarms and padded estimates work because they replace the perception you do not have.

### What is ADHD waiting mode?

It is when an upcoming appointment prevents you from doing anything else beforehand, because you do not trust yourself to keep track of time. Hours can disappear this way. Setting reliable alarms so your brain can stop guarding the time is the most common fix.

### Do analogue clocks really help?

Many people with ADHD find them easier, because the remaining time is visible as space on the clock face rather than as a number you have to subtract. It is a cheap thing to try. Visual countdown timers work on the same principle.

### What should I try first?

Leaving alarms, set backwards from the time you need to walk out the door. That single change prevents more lateness than anything else. [The Late-Diagnosed ADHD Workbook](/books/late-diagnosed-adhd-workbook/) covers time blindness as part of its section on executive function.
