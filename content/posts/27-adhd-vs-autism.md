Most adults who start looking into this have already noticed the problem. They read a description of ADHD and recognise themselves. Then they read a description of autism and recognise themselves there too. The lists overlap enough that recognising yourself in one does not rule out the other.

That is not a failure of attention on your part. The two conditions share a great deal, they frequently occur together, and some of the clearest differences are about why a behaviour happens rather than what it looks like.

## Key takeaways

- The same behaviour often has a different cause in ADHD than in autism, and the cause is what distinguishes them.
- ADHD tends toward novelty and variety; autism tends toward predictability and depth.
- Social difficulty differs: missing the timing versus missing the unwritten rules.
- Sensory differences and a need for sameness point more toward autism; impulsivity and time blindness point more toward ADHD.
- Having both is common, which is why so many people see themselves in both lists.

## The same behaviour, different reason

This is the most useful lens I have found, and it is the one most checklists leave out.

Take interrupting people. With ADHD it is usually impulsivity: the thought will evaporate if it is not said now, so it comes out. With autism it is more often about conversational timing, not reading the subtle cues that mark a gap, or being so engaged with the topic that the social choreography fades.

Take being overwhelmed in a supermarket. With ADHD that is often too many competing things to attend to, with attention bouncing between them. With autism it is more often the sensory load itself: the lighting, the noise, the number of choices on the shelf.

Take rigidity around plans. An autistic person may need the plan because a change is genuinely costly to process. Someone with ADHD may cling to a plan because they know that without it the day will evaporate, or may abandon it entirely because the plan has become boring.

Take losing track of time. With ADHD it tends to be a weak felt sense of time passing. With autism it is more often that a deep interest has absorbed everything, and the clock was never in view.

The outward behaviour is the same. The mechanism is not.

> Checklists compare behaviours. The two conditions are easier to separate by asking what the behaviour is doing for you.

## Where they pull in opposite directions

A few patterns tend to separate more cleanly.

Novelty versus sameness is the big one. ADHD is drawn toward new, interesting and stimulating things, and finds repetition genuinely hard to tolerate. Autism tends toward predictability, familiar routines, the same meal, the same route. Someone who is both will feel the contradiction constantly, which is its own clue.

Social difficulty differs in texture. Many autistic adults describe missing the unwritten rules: not knowing how long to hold eye contact, or how direct is too direct, and learning those things explicitly rather than absorbing them. Many ADHD adults describe knowing the rules perfectly and breaking them anyway, through interrupting, over-talking, or forgetting to reply for three weeks.

Sensory experience is weighted toward autism. Sensory sensitivity exists in ADHD too, but a lifelong, pervasive pattern of sensory needs shaping your clothing, food and environment points more strongly toward autism.

Interests differ in shape. ADHD interest is often intense and short-lived: a new hobby every few months, with the equipment from the last three in a cupboard. Autistic special interests tend to be deep and durable, sometimes lifelong, and are a reliable source of regulation rather than novelty.

Impulsivity and time blindness are more characteristic of ADHD. Need for routine, difficulty with transitions and distress at unexpected change are more characteristic of autism.

## Why so many people see both

Because for a lot of people, both apply.

The two co-occur often enough that the combination has its own informal name, AuDHD, and diagnostic rules in several countries used to prohibit recording both, which left many adults with half an explanation. If you recognise yourself in both descriptions and the contradictions feel familiar, that is worth taking seriously rather than treating as confusion.

There is also a masking problem. An autistic adult who learned to perform social behaviour very well may look ADHD from outside, because what shows is the disorganisation rather than the social effort. And someone with ADHD who built heavy routines to compensate can look autistic, because what shows is the rigidity.

## What this is not

This is not a diagnostic tool, and I am not a clinician. The distinctions above are the ones that helped me understand my own experience and the ones I see other late-identified adults find useful. They are not criteria.

A clinician separating these will look at developmental history, how things presented in childhood, how difficulties show up across different settings, and what else might explain them. That is a different process from recognising yourself in a description, and it is worth doing if you want an answer you can act on.

## What to do with the question

If you are trying to work out which, the most useful preparation is to collect examples rather than labels. For each thing you struggle with, write down what actually happens and what it feels like from inside. Did you interrupt because you would lose the thought, or because you did not notice the pause? Is the supermarket hard because of the noise or because of the choices?

That detail is what a good assessment works from, and it is the part you cannot reconstruct in the room. The article on [getting assessed as an adult](/blog/adult-adhd-autism-assessment/) covers the rest of the preparation.

## Common questions

### Can you have both ADHD and autism?

Yes, and it is common. The informal term for it is AuDHD. Diagnostic rules in several countries once prevented clinicians from recording both, so many adults were given whichever was noticed first. Those rules have changed, although practice has not caught up everywhere.

### What is the main difference between ADHD and autism?

In broad terms, ADHD centres on attention, impulsivity and the executive functions that organise behaviour, while autism centres on social communication, sensory processing and a need for predictability. In practice the clearest difference is often why a given behaviour happens rather than what it looks like.

### Can ADHD be mistaken for autism?

Yes, in both directions. Someone with ADHD who built heavy routines to compensate can appear rigid, and an autistic adult who masks well can appear simply disorganised. Social difficulties, sensory sensitivity and trouble with focus occur in both, which is why assessment relies on developmental history rather than a symptom list alone.

### Which should I get assessed for first?

That is usually a question for the clinician rather than something to decide in advance. Describe everything you experience rather than steering toward one label, because the overlap is exactly what they are trained to untangle. If you have already been diagnosed with one and the strategies only partly work, the other is worth raising.
