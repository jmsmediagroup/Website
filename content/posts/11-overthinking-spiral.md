It is almost always something small. A phrase you used in a meeting. The way you said goodbye to someone at a party in 2014. A message you sent that got a short reply.

During the day these would not survive five seconds of attention. At two in the morning they are the most important thing that has ever happened, and you will examine them from every angle for an hour, reaching no conclusion, with work in six hours.

## Key takeaways

- The spiral arrives at night because the distractions that were suppressing it are gone.
- It is rumination, which feels like problem-solving but produces no decisions.
- Arguing with the content extends it, because engaging is what keeps it running.
- Interrupting it needs enough cognitive load to occupy the loop, rather than relaxation.
- Phone use in bed reliably makes it worse, and it is also the hardest thing to change.

## Why night

Nothing about the thoughts is nocturnal. What changes is everything else.

All day your attention has been occupied. Work, noise, conversation, music, screens. For an ADHD brain that is understimulated by default, this is a continuous supply of input, and it keeps the loop suppressed by crowding it out.

Then you lie down in a dark, quiet room with no input at all. The understimulation becomes acute, and your brain goes looking for something to process. What it finds is the unresolved material: the social interaction with an ambiguous outcome, the thing you said that might have landed badly, the task you have not started.

Ambiguous social information is the preferred fuel, because it cannot be resolved. A problem with an answer gets solved and released. A question like did that person find me annoying has no available answer, so it can be worked indefinitely, which is exactly what makes it so sticky.

Tiredness makes it worse rather than better. The inhibition that would normally let you drop a thought is at its weakest when you are exhausted, which is why the same thought you dismissed at eleven is immovable at two.

## It feels productive, which is the trap

The spiral presents itself as working on something. You are analysing, considering, examining the evidence. It has the texture of problem-solving.

The difference is that problem-solving terminates. It produces a decision, an action, or a conclusion that you can act on tomorrow. Rumination produces another lap. If you have gone round the same material four times and arrived nowhere, that is not analysis, whatever it feels like.

This matters because it tells you which tool to use. You do not need to think harder about the thing. You need to stop the mechanism, which is a different task entirely.

> If you have examined it four times and reached nothing, a fifth pass is not going to be the one.

## Why reasoning with it fails

The intuitive response is to answer the thought. Work out whether you really did say something awkward, decide it was fine, and move on.

This almost never works, for two reasons.

The first is that resolving one item does not close the loop. It just frees capacity for the next item, and there is always a next item. Fourteen years of social interactions is a deep archive. You cannot clear it by adjudicating entries one at a time.

The second is that engaging is what sustains it. The loop needs something to process, and your attention is the thing it is processing with. Arguing with the content is feeding it.

Trying to force the thoughts out is similarly counterproductive. Suppression requires monitoring whether the thought is present, which means holding it in mind, which is the thing you were trying to stop.

## What actually interrupts it

The approach that works is giving the loop something else to chew on, at the right difficulty.

It has to be demanding enough to occupy you. Relaxation usually fails, because a calm, low-input activity leaves exactly the kind of spare capacity the spiral is using. What works better is something that requires mild, continuous cognitive effort: an audiobook or podcast you have to follow, counting backwards in sevens, naming things in a category, describing a familiar route in detail. Boring enough for sleep, occupying enough to displace the loop.

Writing it down works better than it sounds. Not journaling, and not analysing. Three lines, on paper, by the bed. Something like: worried I was short with Dan, will check tomorrow. The effect is that the thought stops having to be held, because holding it is partly about not wanting to lose it.

Getting up helps when twenty minutes have passed with no progress. Lying in the dark reinforces the association between bed and spiralling. Sit somewhere else, do something dull and dimly lit, go back when you feel sleepy. This is slow to pay off and it does pay off.

A deliberate worry slot during the day sounds artificial and works for a lot of people. Fifteen minutes, same time, where you address the things that keep surfacing. It gives the loop a legitimate appointment, which makes a surprising difference to how insistent it is at night.

And the physical things matter more than they should. Cold room, consistent timing, less caffeine later than you think is reasonable. None of this is exciting advice, and it moves the baseline.

## The phone

Worth addressing on its own, because it is the most common accelerant and the hardest to give up.

Phones at night do two incompatible things. They do occupy the loop, which is why reaching for one feels like relief. They also supply an endless stream of new, mildly activating material, which keeps you awake and frequently adds fresh items for the spiral to work with.

The other pattern here is revenge bedtime procrastination: staying up because the evening is the only part of the day that belongs to you, and going to sleep means it ends and tomorrow starts. That is not a sleep hygiene problem. It is usually a sign that there is not enough unclaimed time in your day, and the fix is upstream of bedtime.

If removing the phone entirely is not realistic, charging it across the room is the version most people can actually sustain. The requirement to stand up introduces enough friction to break the automatic reach.

## Common questions

### Why does overthinking get worse at night with ADHD?

Mostly because the external stimulation that was suppressing it disappears when you lie down. An understimulated brain in a dark, silent room generates its own input, and unresolved social material is the most available source. Reduced inhibition when tired makes the thoughts harder to drop than they were earlier in the day.

### Is this anxiety or ADHD?

They overlap heavily and frequently coexist. Rumination is strongly associated with anxiety, and ADHD contributes understimulation, poor inhibition and a large stock of unresolved social moments. If the worry is persistent, occurs across most areas of your life, or is affecting your functioning during the day, that is worth discussing with a professional rather than attributing to ADHD and managing alone.

### Does melatonin or medication help?

That is a question for a prescriber, and the answer depends on your situation. What I can say is that sleep timing is genuinely different for many ADHD adults, and it is a reasonable thing to raise at an appointment rather than something to solve purely with willpower and routine.

### Why do I remember embarrassing things from years ago?

Because they were never resolved, and unresolved social information is the most reliable fuel for this. Memories with a strong emotional charge and no conclusion stay available, and the quiet of the night is when your brain goes looking for something to process.

### What should I try first?

An audiobook on a sleep timer, and three lines on paper by the bed. The combination displaces the loop and removes the need to keep holding things in mind. [The ADHD Overthinking Reset](https://www.amazon.com/dp/B0GDGK3RLK) goes into the interrupt techniques in more depth.
