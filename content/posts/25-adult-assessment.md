Deciding to seek an assessment as an adult usually comes after a long stretch of suspicion. You have read the descriptions, recognised yourself, wondered whether you are imagining it, and finally decided you want to know.

The process can feel opaque, slow and expensive. This is a practical overview of how it tends to work. Details change, and they differ a lot between countries and providers, so treat the [official sources in the resources directory](/resources/#assessment) as the authority for where you live.

## Key takeaways

- Assessment usually starts with your doctor or primary care provider, who may refer you to a specialist.
- Assessments typically involve detailed interviews about your current life and your childhood, plus questionnaires.
- Preparing specific, concrete examples makes a large difference to how useful the assessment is.
- In England, Right to Choose can let you pick a different NHS provider when local waits are long.
- Waits can be long, and there is a great deal you can do in the meantime.

## Where it starts

For most people the first step is an appointment with a GP or primary care doctor. In the UK, the NHS describes the GP asking about your symptoms and how they affect your life, considering other possible explanations, and then deciding whether to refer you for a specialist assessment.

In the United States, people may start with a primary care doctor, or go directly to a psychiatrist or psychologist. Insurance coverage and costs vary a great deal, so it is worth checking what your plan covers before you book.

Private assessments are available in many countries and can be faster, at a cost. If you go private, check what happens afterwards. In the UK, for example, people who want their GP to take over prescribing after a private ADHD diagnosis usually need a shared care agreement, and ADHD UK suggests speaking to your GP about it before the private assessment rather than after.

## Right to Choose, in England

In England, you have a legal right to choose who carries out your NHS ADHD or autism assessment. If your local wait is very long, your GP can refer you to another NHS-approved provider. ADHD UK has a clear guide to how this works, linked in the [resources directory](/resources/#assessment).

## What an assessment involves

The details vary, but the general shape is similar.

For ADHD, expect a detailed clinical interview about how you function now, across work, home, relationships and daily tasks, and about your childhood, because ADHD is present from early in life. You will usually fill in questionnaires. Some clinicians also ask for input from someone who knew you as a child, such as a parent or sibling, or for old school reports.

For autism, expect interviews about your social communication, sensory experiences, routines and interests, and your developmental history, again often with input from someone who knew you young. Some assessments include structured observational tasks.

Clinicians are also checking for other explanations, and for conditions that commonly occur alongside, such as anxiety or depression.

## How to prepare

This is the part most people underestimate.

Write down specific examples. "I struggle with organisation" is less useful than "I have paid late fees on bills I could afford at least six times this year" or "I left three jobs in five years after the first six months". Concrete examples give the clinician something to work with.

Cover your childhood. Ask family what you were like. Find school reports if you can; comments like "could do better if she applied herself" or "very quiet, keeps to herself" can be informative.

Note what you do to cope. Masking and compensation can hide difficulties, so describe the effort behind things that look fine from outside.

Bring a list of questions, because you will forget them in the room.

> Bring examples, not adjectives. "Late fees six times this year" says more than "disorganised".

## While you wait

Waits can be long. Waiting does not have to mean doing nothing.

Many of the practical strategies that help with ADHD or autism do not require a diagnosis: sensory adjustments, external reminders, body doubling, automating bills. Peer communities welcome people who are questioning. And keeping notes while you wait builds the material that will make your assessment more useful.

## Common questions

### How do I get assessed for ADHD as an adult?

Usually by starting with your GP or primary care doctor, who may refer you to a specialist. Private assessments are also available in many places. The process differs by country, so check the official guidance where you live, which is linked in the resources directory.

### What happens in an adult autism assessment?

Typically a detailed interview about social communication, sensory experiences, routines and interests, plus your developmental history, often with input from someone who knew you as a child. Some assessments include structured observation. Clinicians also consider other explanations and co-occurring conditions.

### What is Right to Choose?

In England, Right to Choose is the legal right to choose who carries out your NHS ADHD or autism assessment. If local waits are long, your GP can refer you to another NHS-approved provider. It applies in England specifically.

### How can I prepare for an ADHD or autism assessment?

Write down specific, concrete examples of how things affect you now, gather information about your childhood, including school reports and family memories, describe the effort behind things that look fine, and bring a list of questions.

### Do I need an assessment to get help?

No. Many practical strategies and peer communities do not require a diagnosis. A formal assessment can matter for medication, workplace adjustments and some services. Before booking, [what online tests can tell you](/blog/online-adhd-autism-tests/) explains how to read self-screeners.
