Two things get mixed up constantly here, and untangling them is most of the work.

There are the diagnostic levels, 1 to 3, which describe how much support a person needs. And there are the older functioning labels, high and low, which are not diagnostic terms at all and which most autistic adults I know actively dislike. They are not the same thing, and the difference matters more than it sounds.

## Key takeaways

- The levels describe support needs, not severity of autism or intelligence.
- Support needs fluctuate, so a single permanent level describes most people badly.
- "High functioning" describes how someone appears to others, which is a different thing entirely.
- Appearing to cope is often the result of masking, and masking has a cost nobody else sees.
- Most autistic adults prefer describing specific needs over any single label.

## What the levels actually mean

Current diagnostic practice describes autism in three levels, assigned according to how much support someone requires. In broad terms:

| Level | Usual description |
| --- | --- |
| Level 1 | Requiring support |
| Level 2 | Requiring substantial support |
| Level 3 | Requiring very substantial support |

The important word in all three is support. The levels are a statement about what a person needs from their environment, not a measure of how autistic they are, how intelligent they are, or how much they are capable of.

They are also assigned separately for social communication and for restricted or repetitive behaviours, so someone can need very different amounts of support in each. A single number is already a simplification of a two-part judgement.

## Why a fixed level describes people badly

Support needs move. They move with the environment, with how much sleep you have had, with whether you are in [autistic burnout](/blog/autistic-burnout-vs-adhd-burnout-vs-depression/), with how much you have had to mask that week.

The same person can manage a familiar office on a quiet Tuesday and be unable to order food in a loud restaurant that evening. Neither is their "real" level. Both are real.

This is the main practical complaint autistic adults make about levels: they get recorded once, usually during an assessment on an unrepresentative day, and then follow you around as though they were a fixed property. A level assigned when you were coping can make it harder to get support later when you are not.

> Support needs are a description of a moment, not a permanent setting. Most people's move a great deal.

## The problem with "high functioning"

Functioning labels are not diagnostic categories. They came into general use informally, and they describe something different from the levels: how well someone appears to be managing, as judged from outside.

That difference is the whole problem.

Being called high functioning usually means you look fine to other people. It frequently means you have become good at [masking](/blog/autistic-masking-and-what-it-costs/), which is exhausting, invisible, and strongly associated with burnout. The label then becomes a reason to withhold support, because a person who looks fine is assumed not to need any. Plenty of late-identified adults have been told some version of "but you seem to be doing well" by someone deciding what they qualify for.

Low functioning is worse in the other direction. It tends to attach to people who communicate differently or do not speak, and it is routinely taken to mean an absence of ability or understanding, which is not what it describes and often not true.

Both labels share the same flaw: they measure how legible someone is to non-autistic observers. That is a fact about the observer at least as much as about the autistic person.

## What autistic adults tend to prefer

Specifics, in my experience, over any label.

Saying "I need written instructions and I cannot process speech in a noisy room" tells someone what to do. Saying "I am level 1" or "I am high functioning" tells them almost nothing useful, and in the second case may tell them something actively misleading.

Where a single term is needed, support needs is the phrasing that has largely replaced functioning language: low support needs, high support needs, and the acknowledgement that these vary. It is not perfect either, but it at least points at the thing that matters.

The levels still have a real administrative use. In many places they determine access to services and funding, which is exactly why they are worth understanding rather than dismissing. Knowing what a level is for, and knowing it was assigned on one day in one room, is the useful combination.

## Common questions

### What are the three levels of autism?

Current diagnostic practice describes level 1 as requiring support, level 2 as requiring substantial support, and level 3 as requiring very substantial support. They are assigned separately for social communication and for restricted or repetitive behaviours, and they describe support needs rather than severity or intelligence.

### Is "high functioning autism" a diagnosis?

No. It is an informal description rather than a diagnostic category, and it is not part of current diagnostic criteria. It describes how well someone appears to be managing from the outside, which is often a measure of how effectively they mask rather than of how much support they need.

### Why do autistic people dislike functioning labels?

Mainly because they describe how legible someone is to others rather than what they actually need. "High functioning" is often used to deny support to people who are coping at considerable hidden cost, and "low functioning" is routinely taken to mean an absence of ability or understanding, which is not what it means.

### Can your autism level change?

The recorded level usually does not change without a reassessment, but the support someone needs certainly does, with environment, health, stress and burnout. This mismatch is one of the main criticisms autistic adults make of a fixed level.

### What should I say instead?

Describe the specific support you need: written instructions, quieter spaces, advance notice of change, more processing time. It is more useful to whoever is trying to help than any label, and it avoids being judged on how well you appear to be managing.
