Autistic meltdown or panic attack? Telling them apart

Meltdowns and panic attacks can look alike from outside and need different responses. What distinguishes them, and what helps during each.

From outside they can look the same: someone distressed, crying, struggling to speak, needing to get out of the room. The responses that help are close to opposite, which is why the distinction is worth understanding even though it is not always clean.

I want to be careful here. Panic attacks are a clinical matter, autistic people experience them at least as often as anyone else, and the two can happen in the same episode. What follows is how the experiences tend to differ, not a way to diagnose yourself.

Key takeaways

  • Meltdowns are usually driven by accumulated overload; panic attacks are usually driven by fear.
  • Overload tends to build over hours or days, while panic often arrives abruptly.
  • Panic attacks typically carry strong physical fear symptoms and a sense of dread or danger.
  • Reassurance helps panic; reducing sensory input helps a meltdown.
  • They can occur together, and a clinician is the right person to untangle it.

What tends to drive each

A meltdown is generally the end of a load problem. Sensory input, social effort, masking, uncertainty and demands accumulate across hours or days until there is no capacity left, and control of your responses goes. The final trigger is often trivial, which is one of the recognisable features: a dropped glass or a changed plan sets off something wildly out of proportion to it, because the real cause was everything that came before.

A panic attack is generally a fear response. The body's alarm system fires, often without a proportionate external cause, and produces intense physical symptoms: racing heart, shortness of breath, chest tightness, dizziness, sweating, and frequently a sense of dread or a conviction that something terrible is happening.

That difference in driver is the most useful thing to hold onto. One is a system that has run out of capacity. The other is a system that has sounded an alarm.

They are not mutually exclusive, and an episode can move from one into the other.

Differences people describe

The build. Meltdowns usually have a run-up, even if you did not notice it: rising irritability, sounds getting sharper, stimming increasing, an urge to leave. Panic attacks more often arrive suddenly, sometimes with no warning at all.

The physical signature. The cardiac and respiratory symptoms, the feeling of not being able to breathe, and the fear of dying or losing control are characteristic of panic. Meltdowns can involve physical distress, but the specific terror-plus-heart-racing combination is more typical of panic.

What is in your head. During panic there is usually fear with content: something bad is happening or about to. During a meltdown people more often describe being past thought entirely, flooded, unable to process language, with no specific fear attached.

What preceded it. If you can trace a day or a week of accumulating demand, that points toward overload. If it came out of a calm afternoon, that points more toward panic.

What helps. This is the most practical difference. Panic generally responds to grounding, slowed breathing and reassurance that you are safe. A meltdown generally does not respond to reassurance, and talking at someone in one usually makes it worse. What helps is less: less noise, less light, fewer people, fewer words.

With panic, being talked to calmly often helps. With a meltdown, being talked to at all is usually more input than the system can take.

I would treat the often repeated claim that meltdowns last longer than panic attacks with some caution. Both vary enormously, and duration is a weak way to tell them apart compared with what caused the episode and what helps during it.

They are not mutually exclusive

Plenty of autistic people experience both, sometimes close together. Sensory overload is frightening, and fear is an entirely reasonable response to feeling your control slipping in public. An episode can begin as overload and become panic, or the reverse.

Autistic adults also experience anxiety disorders at high rates, which is worth knowing if you are trying to make sense of recurring episodes. The fact that you are autistic does not mean every episode is a meltdown.

What to do during each

If it looks like overload, reduce input. Leave if you can. Headphones, a quiet room, a car, a dim space. Fewer words from anyone present. Recovery usually takes longer than the episode and may include a headache, exhaustion or emotional flatness for a day or more.

If it looks like panic, grounding tends to help: slower breathing, naming things you can see, feeling something solid, and a reminder that the feeling will pass. Panic attacks are intensely unpleasant and generally not dangerous, though that is much easier to hold onto when someone else says it.

If you are supporting someone and cannot tell which it is, the safest default is to reduce the input, stay nearby, say very little, avoid touching them unless you know they want it, and make sure they are safe. That is unlikely to make either worse.

When to involve a professional

If you are having recurring episodes of either kind, or if fear of the next one is shaping where you go and what you do, that is worth raising with a doctor. Panic disorder is treatable, and treatment works a good deal better than endurance.

If any episode involves thoughts of harming yourself, please contact a crisis line or emergency services. The resources directory lists free crisis support by country.

Common questions

What is the difference between an autistic meltdown and a panic attack?

A meltdown is generally a response to accumulated sensory, social or emotional overload, while a panic attack is generally a fear response with strong physical symptoms such as a racing heart and shortness of breath. They can look similar from outside, and the clearest difference is usually what caused the episode and what helps during it.

Can autistic people have panic attacks?

Yes. Being autistic does not make panic attacks less likely, and anxiety disorders are commonly reported among autistic adults. An episode can also begin as overload and develop into panic.

How can I tell which one I am having?

Afterwards, look at what preceded it. A day or week of accumulating demand points toward overload. A sudden onset from a calm state with intense physical fear symptoms points toward panic. What helped is also informative: reassurance tends to help panic, while reducing input tends to help a meltdown.

Does breathing exercises help a meltdown?

Often not, and being coached through breathing can add demand at a moment when processing language is already hard. Grounding and breathing are more associated with panic. For a meltdown, reducing sensory input and giving space tends to help more.

What should I do afterwards?

Treat recovery as part of the event rather than a separate thing. Rest, low input, and no analysis until you have capacity again. The Late-Diagnosed Autistic Workbook covers reading your energy before it reaches zero, which is where most of the prevention lives.
