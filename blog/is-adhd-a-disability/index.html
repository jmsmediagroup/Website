Is ADHD a disability? And is autism one?

Whether ADHD or autism counts as a disability depends on the law where you live and how much it affects you, not on the diagnosis itself.

People usually ask this for a practical reason. They want to know whether they can ask for adjustments at work, whether they are protected if things go badly, or whether they qualify for support. Occasionally they are asking something more personal: whether calling it a disability is accurate, or an overstatement.

The legal answer and the identity answer are different questions, and they deserve separating. I am not a lawyer, and the rules differ by country, so what follows is orientation rather than advice, with the official sources linked.

Key takeaways

  • In most legal systems it depends on how much it affects your daily life, not on the diagnosis itself.
  • The same diagnosis can be a disability for one person and not for another.
  • In the UK, autism is not a learning disability, though the two can occur together.
  • Being covered legally does not require you to identify as disabled, and identifying as disabled does not require a diagnosis.
  • Workplace adjustments are often available without any of this being settled.

The law asks about effect, not label

This is the part that surprises people. In most frameworks the question is not whether you have a named condition. It is whether an impairment has a substantial and long-term effect on your ability to do normal daily activities.

In the UK, that is the test set out in the Equality Act 2010. ADHD and autism are not automatically listed as disabilities; they qualify when they meet that test, which many people's do. In the United States, the Americans with Disabilities Act works on a similar principle, asking whether an impairment substantially limits a major life activity. Its employment protections apply to employers with 15 or more employees.

So two people with the same diagnosis can land differently, and the same person can land differently at different points in their life. That is not a loophole; it follows directly from a test about effect.

What this means practically is that the useful evidence is not the diagnosis letter on its own. It is the specific account of what is affected: that you cannot reliably track deadlines without external systems, that a noisy open-plan office makes your work unsustainable, that you lose a day after a demanding meeting.

The diagnosis opens the conversation. What decides it is the description of what is actually affected.

Autism is not a learning disability

Worth stating plainly, because it is asked constantly and the terms differ between countries.

In the UK, "learning disability" means a reduced intellectual ability alongside difficulty with everyday activities, which is a specific thing. Autism is a different thing. An autistic person may also have a learning disability, and many do not. Autistic people span the full range of intellectual ability.

The confusion is worse because American usage differs: "learning disability" there usually means something closer to what the UK calls a specific learning difficulty, such as dyslexia. So the same phrase means different things depending on who is saying it, which is worth checking before assuming you have been understood.

Benefits and disability payments are a separate question with their own tests, and those tests are stricter and country-specific. I would not generalise about them here.

You can usually ask without settling any of this

Something that gets lost in the legal framing: most of the useful adjustments are things a reasonable employer will do if asked, without anyone deciding your legal status first.

Written instructions instead of verbal ones. A quieter desk. Noise-cancelling headphones. Agendas before meetings. Flexibility about when you do focused work. None of these requires a diagnosis, a tribunal, or the word disability. They are ordinary requests, and framing them around what helps you work well goes further than framing them around entitlement.

Formal protection matters when things go wrong, or when an employer refuses. For day-to-day functioning, asking plainly is usually the faster route. ADHD at work and autistic at work go through the specifics.

The identity question

Separately from any of it, people ask whether they should think of themselves as disabled.

That one is yours, and reasonable people land in different places. Some find the word accurate and clarifying: it names the fact that the difficulty is real and partly structural rather than a personal failing. Others find it does not describe their experience, or carries associations they do not want.

The social model of disability is worth knowing about here, because it reframes the question usefully. On that view, a person is disabled less by the condition itself than by environments built for other people. A noisy open-plan office is not a neutral setting that you happen to struggle in; it is a design choice that disables some people and not others.

You can hold that view, or not, and still ask for the adjustment. Nothing practical depends on resolving it.

Official sources

  • Acas on reasonable adjustments, for the UK employer duty under the Equality Act 2010.
  • Access to Work, for UK funding, which covers England, Scotland and Wales.
  • The Job Accommodation Network, for free US guidance on workplace accommodations.
  • EEOC disability resources, for US employment protections.

Common questions

Is ADHD legally a disability?

It can be, and in many cases it is, but it is not automatic. Most legal systems ask whether an impairment has a substantial and long-term effect on normal daily activities rather than whether you have a particular diagnosis. The UK uses that test under the Equality Act 2010, and the US asks a similar question under the ADA.

Is autism a disability?

The same answer applies: it depends on effect rather than on the label, and for many autistic people it meets the legal test. Separately from the law, whether you describe yourself as disabled is a personal decision, and autistic people differ on it.

Is autism a learning disability?

Not in UK terms. A learning disability there means reduced intellectual ability together with difficulty managing everyday activities. Autism is distinct, and autistic people span the full range of intellectual ability, although some also have a learning disability. American usage of the phrase differs, which causes a lot of confusion.

Do I need a diagnosis to get adjustments at work?

Not to ask. Many useful adjustments are reasonable requests in any workplace. Formal legal protection usually depends on meeting the legal test where you live, and evidence helps there. Official sources such as Acas or the Job Accommodation Network explain the specifics.

Does calling it a disability mean I am not capable?

No. The legal test is about whether something has a substantial effect on daily activities, not about ability or potential. Plenty of people who meet the test work at a high level, often precisely because the right adjustments are in place.
