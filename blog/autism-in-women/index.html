Autism in women: camouflage, late diagnosis, and the wrong labels

Why many autistic women are identified late or given other diagnoses first, how camouflaging hides autism, and what tends to change once you know.

By J.M. Struijk · 21 October 2026 · 4 min read

Many autistic women describe the same strange experience on finding out: everything suddenly makes sense, and at the same time they cannot believe nobody noticed. They were the quiet, well-behaved girl, the one with intense interests, the one who studied other people carefully and copied what worked. They passed, and passing was the problem.

Key takeaways

  • Many autistic women are identified late because they camouflage effectively and do not match older descriptions of autism.
  • Camouflaging works socially and costs a great deal, and it is a common route into burnout.
  • Women are often given other diagnoses first, sometimes several, before autism is considered.
  • Special interests may be overlooked because they look socially ordinary.
  • Recognition usually brings relief, grief and a period of reinterpreting your own history.

Why so many are missed

Older descriptions of autism were built mainly from observations of boys, and many autistic women simply do not look like that picture. A girl who is socially motivated, who wants friends and works hard to keep them, does not match the stereotype of someone uninterested in people.

Camouflaging is the bigger reason. Many autistic girls learn early to watch other people closely and imitate them: expressions, phrases, interests, the right amount of eye contact. They rehearse conversations. They script. Done well enough, it hides autism from teachers, parents and clinicians, and often from the girl herself.

Interests can be overlooked too. An intense, absorbing interest in horses, a band, a book series or psychology looks age-appropriate, so its depth and intensity go unremarked.

The wrong labels

Many autistic women describe receiving other diagnoses first. Anxiety and depression are the most common, and they are often real: years of camouflaging and not fitting in take a toll. Eating difficulties, sometimes linked to sensory issues or a need for control and sameness, are also reported. Some are given personality disorder diagnoses before anyone considers autism.

The problem is not that these conditions never apply. Sometimes they do, alongside autism. The problem is when they are treated as the whole explanation, so the underlying difference goes unaddressed and the treatment works less well than it should.

Being good at blending in is not the same as not being autistic. It is often the reason nobody looked.

What it often looks like in adulthood

These are patterns, not criteria, and only an assessment can tell you what applies.

Exhaustion after social time that you genuinely enjoyed. A sense of running a social script and watching yourself perform it. Sensory sensitivities that you have always managed quietly: clothing labels, certain sounds, crowded places, particular food textures. A strong need for routine that looks like organisation from outside. Difficulty knowing what you feel until later. Deep, long-lasting interests. A history of friendships that ended without you understanding why.

And often burnout: periods where the effort of camouflaging became unsustainable and everything, including things you used to manage, became too much.

After recognition

Relief usually comes first. Then grief, for the years spent believing you were a badly made version of everyone else. Then a long period of rereading your own history.

Unmasking, gradually and where it is safe, is part of what tends to help, along with sensory changes that make daily life less costly, and finding other autistic women whose experiences mirror yours. Many describe autistic community as the first place they did not have to translate.

Common questions

Why are autistic women diagnosed later?

Many camouflage effectively and do not match older descriptions of autism, which were built mostly from observations of boys. Socially motivated girls with age-appropriate interests often went unnoticed, and their difficulties were attributed to anxiety, shyness or sensitivity instead.

What is camouflaging?

Camouflaging, also called masking, is hiding autistic traits and performing expected behaviour to fit in: copying others, scripting conversations, forcing eye contact and suppressing stimming. It can work socially but is very tiring to sustain, and it is closely associated with burnout.

What are common misdiagnoses for autistic women?

Many autistic women describe being diagnosed with anxiety or depression first, and some with eating disorders or personality disorders. These conditions can also occur alongside autism. The concern is when they are treated as the full explanation and autism is never considered.

Can I be autistic if I have friends and make eye contact?

Yes. Many autistic people have friends, want social connection and can make eye contact, often through learned effort. What matters in assessment is the underlying experience and its cost, not whether you can perform expected behaviour.

Where should I start?

Reading accounts from autistic women and considering an assessment if you want formal confirmation. The articles on getting assessed as an adult and masking go further, and The Late-Diagnosed Autistic Workbook is written for after you find out.
