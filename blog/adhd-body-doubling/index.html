Body doubling: why ADHD tasks get easier when someone else is in the room

Body doubling means working with someone present who is not helping. Why it helps ADHD adults start tasks, and how to set it up.

By J.M. Struijk · 21 October 2026 · 5 min read
Filed underEveryday ADHD: focus, time and feelings

For years I could not do my tax paperwork. I could do it, technically. I understood every form. I just could not start, and it sat on the desk for weeks, getting heavier.

Then someone sat on the other end of the sofa reading a book, and I finished the whole lot in an afternoon. They did not help. They did not even look at it. They were just there.

That is body doubling, and it sounds far too simple to work. For a lot of ADHD adults it is the single most reliable way of getting started on anything.

Key takeaways

  • Body doubling is doing a task while another person is present, without them helping.
  • It works for starting and continuing tasks, especially boring or avoided ones.
  • The other person can be in the room, on a video call, or a stranger on a co-working stream.
  • Matching the format to the task matters more than finding the perfect partner.
  • It is a legitimate tool, not a crutch, and needing it says nothing about your competence.

What it actually is

The definition is short. You do your task. Another person is present while you do it. They are not supervising, teaching or helping. Usually they are doing their own thing.

It can be a partner on the sofa, a friend at the kitchen table, a colleague in a quiet room, a video call where both of you work silently with cameras on, or a recorded video of someone studying. Some people go to a café for the same reason, and get the effect from strangers.

People with ADHD often discover it by accident and then feel slightly embarrassed about it, as though needing an audience to do the washing up is something to hide. It is not. It is a tool that suits a particular brain, the same way glasses suit particular eyes.

Why it works

I want to be careful here, because the honest answer is that nobody has a complete explanation, and plenty of confident ones circulate online. What I can describe is what people with ADHD consistently report.

The presence of another person seems to anchor attention. Alone, the mind drifts, and there is nothing pulling it back. With someone there, there is a gentle external reference point. You notice when you have wandered off, because you are briefly aware of being seen doing nothing.

There is a mild accountability effect, even when the other person is not paying attention. Saying "I'm going to do my emails now" out loud to someone makes it slightly more real than deciding it in your head.

It also makes the task feel less lonely. A lot of avoided tasks are avoided partly because they are isolating and grim. Another person nearby changes the emotional temperature, and the task becomes something you are doing in company rather than alone in a room with a pile.

And it breaks the starting problem. The hardest moment in most ADHD tasks is the transition from not doing it to doing it. A body double turns that into an appointment: we start at two.

Ways to do it

In person is the strongest version for most people. Agree a time, sit in the same space, each work on your own thing. A few words at the start about what you are each doing helps. Then stop talking.

Virtual body doubling works surprisingly well. A video call with a friend, both muted, both working. Cameras on seems to matter for many people, even though nobody is watching.

Structured co-working sessions are useful when you do not have a willing friend. There are services and community sessions built around this, where strangers join timed blocks, state a goal at the start, and report back at the end. The structure adds an extra layer of commitment.

Recorded videos of people working are the lightest version. They help some people a lot and others not at all. They are free and available at three in the morning, which counts for something.

Even a phone call can work. Some people ring a friend, put the phone on speaker, and both clean their kitchens.

Making it work

Match the format to the task. Admin and paperwork often need a quiet, focused double. Physical tasks like cleaning work well with a chatty one, or with someone on a call doing the same chore.

Say the task out loud at the start. Specifically. "I'm going to open the three letters from the bank" works better than "I'm going to do admin". Reporting back at the end, even briefly, closes the loop.

Keep the talking to a minimum during focused tasks. Body doubling turns into socialising very easily, which is pleasant and produces nothing.

Schedule it. The value is partly in the appointment. "We'll do it some time" has the same problem as every other ADHD intention.

Reciprocate if you can. Being someone else's body double costs very little and makes it easier to ask again.

When it does not suit you

For some autistic people, and some AuDHD people, the presence of another person is draining rather than anchoring. Monitoring someone else, even passively, uses up exactly the capacity the task needed. If body doubling leaves you more tired than the task would have, try the lightest version, a silent video or a recorded stream, or skip it.

It can also become a dependency if it is the only way you can start anything. That is not a disaster, but it is worth keeping a few other starting tools alongside it, for the days when nobody is available.

Common questions

Is body doubling a real ADHD strategy?

It is a widely used and widely recommended strategy among people with ADHD and the coaches who work with them. The research base is still small, so it is better described as a well-established practical tool than as a proven treatment. Plenty of people find it works when other things have not.

Does the other person have to be doing the same task?

No. Usually they are doing something completely different. The point is their presence, not their help. Some people find a double working on a similar task slightly more motivating, but it is not necessary.

Does virtual body doubling work as well as in person?

For many people it works well, though in-person tends to be stronger. Cameras on and a brief statement of goals at the start seem to help a virtual session a lot.

Why can I only clean when someone is coming over?

That is closely related. An approaching visitor creates urgency and a sense of being seen, which are two of the things a body double provides. Booking a body doubling session can recreate some of that without needing the guest.

What if I have no one to ask?

Use structured co-working sessions with strangers or recorded study-with-me videos. Both are free or cheap and available at any hour. The Late-Diagnosed ADHD Workbook covers starting tasks and the wall between knowing and doing in more depth.
