Masking, and what it costs

Masking is the constant work of appearing normal, and most people who do it stopped noticing years ago. What it is, why it is so expensive, and how unmasking actually tends to go.

By J.M. Struijk · 21 October 2026 · 6 min read

Masking is the work of appearing normal, and the people doing most of it are usually the least aware of it.

It is not a performance you decide to give. For most late-identified adults it was assembled in childhood out of trial and error, kept because it worked, and run so continuously that it stopped being distinguishable from personality. You cannot drop something you do not know you are holding.

The usual way people find out is by noticing the cost first, and working backwards.

Key takeaways

  • Masking is suppressing natural behaviour and performing expected behaviour, usually automatically.
  • It is effortful in a way that does not register as effort, which is why the exhaustion seems unexplained.
  • It works, which is precisely why it is so hard to give up.
  • It is strongly associated with burnout, anxiety and depression in autistic adults.
  • Unmasking is gradual and situational, and safety has to come first.

What it actually involves

Masking is made of small, constant adjustments.

Rehearsing conversations in advance, including ones that will probably not happen. Monitoring your face in real time to check it is doing the right thing. Making eye contact at measured intervals because you learned the interval, counting rather than feeling it. Suppressing the movement your body wants to make: the rocking, the hand movements, the tapping, usually replaced with something less visible like a foot or a thumb against a finger.

It involves copying. Many late-identified adults describe having assembled a personality out of pieces of other people, sometimes consciously, often from television. Laughing a beat after everyone else because the laugh is a response to the group rather than to the joke. Having scripts for ordinary interactions and panicking when a conversation leaves the script.

It also means hiding what you actually care about. Learning that your interests are too much, so you ration how long you talk about them and watch the other person's face for the point where you should stop.

None of this is dishonesty. It is accommodation, performed unilaterally, usually without acknowledgement.

Why it is so expensive

The cost comes from the fact that it never stops and never becomes free.

Most skills become automatic and cheap with repetition. Masking stays expensive because it requires continuous real-time monitoring. You are running the conversation and simultaneously running a process that checks your own output against a model of what is expected. That second process is the load, and it runs for the entire duration of every social interaction.

On top of that, suppressing stimming removes your main regulation tool at exactly the moment you need it. Stimming is how many autistic people manage sensory and emotional load. Masking means holding that load with the regulator switched off.

The bill arrives afterwards, which is why it is hard to connect to its cause. You were fine at the event. You were fine on the way home. Then you are flat for two days and it looks like nothing happened, because from outside nothing did.

Sustained over years, this is one of the main routes into autistic burnout. It is also associated with anxiety and depression, and with a particular kind of loneliness: being surrounded by people who like a version of you that you built and maintain at considerable expense.

People liking the mask is not the same as being known, and the gap between those two is where the loneliness lives.

Why people keep doing it

Masking works. That has to be said plainly, because advice to simply stop tends to come from people who have not weighed what stopping costs.

It gets you employed. It gets you through interviews, meetings and performance reviews. It keeps you safe in environments where visible difference attracts hostility. For many people it has been the thing standing between them and much worse outcomes.

So it is not a bad habit. It is a strategy that was correct at the time, that has a running cost, and that most people were never given the chance to opt out of. The question worth asking is not whether to mask, but where it is still buying you something and where you are paying for nothing.

How unmasking tends to go

It is slower and more situational than the word implies, and it is not a single event.

It generally starts in private. Letting yourself stim at home. Wearing what is comfortable. Dropping the voice you use on the phone. Noticing, often with some surprise, how much quieter it is in your head without the monitoring running.

Then it extends selectively, usually with one or two people first. This is where most of the fear lives, and the fear is not irrational. Some relationships are built substantially on the masked version, and some of those do not survive. Most people find a smaller number of closer relationships on the other side, which is generally an improvement and does not feel like one at the time.

Work is usually last, and often partial. Plenty of people stay masked professionally and unmask everywhere else, which is a legitimate choice rather than a failure of commitment. Safety is a real consideration, and it is not evenly distributed.

A thing worth preparing for: it often feels worse before it feels better. Many people find their tolerance for masking drops sharply once they can see it. Environments you used to manage become difficult. That is usually the end of a very long unconscious effort rather than a deterioration, although it does not feel that way from inside.

Where to start

Notice first, change nothing. Spend a week observing what you do in social situations. What you suppress, what you perform, what you rehearse. Most people are surprised by the volume once they look.

Pick the cheapest change. Something entirely private, with no social consequence. Stimming in your own home is the usual starting point, and the relief is often immediate and larger than expected.

Work out where masking is still earning its cost. Sometimes it genuinely is, and continuing is the right call. Sometimes you are masking out of habit in a room where nobody would mind, and that is the free ground.

Account for recovery honestly. If a masked event costs you a day, the event takes two days. Planning as though it takes one is how the deficit builds.

Common questions

Do only autistic people mask?

No. People with ADHD mask too, often hiding inattention, impulsivity or the effort of keeping up. Anyone whose natural behaviour has been treated as unacceptable may develop something similar. The autistic version is the most studied and tends to be the most comprehensive, covering social, sensory and communication behaviour at once.

Is masking the same as being polite?

No, although the line can look blurry from outside. Everyone adjusts their behaviour by context. Masking is sustained suppression of natural behaviour and continuous performance of unnatural behaviour, running constantly, with a measurable cost afterwards. Politeness does not usually require two days of recovery.

Should I stop masking at work?

That depends entirely on your workplace, your legal protections, and your financial position, and anyone who answers confidently without knowing those does not know enough. Many people unmask partially, in low-risk ways, and keep the rest. There is no obligation to be visible at your own expense.

Why am I more tired since I found out I was masking?

Because awareness makes it harder to sustain, and because you can now see effort that was previously invisible to you. Most people describe a dip after identification before things improve. It usually reflects the cost becoming visible rather than increasing.

How do I know who I am underneath it?

Slowly, and partly by elimination. It helps to notice what you do when nobody is watching, what you are drawn to when there is no social cost, and what you avoid when avoidance is free.
