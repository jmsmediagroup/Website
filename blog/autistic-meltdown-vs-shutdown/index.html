Autistic meltdown vs shutdown: what is happening, and what helps

Meltdowns and shutdowns are involuntary responses to overload, one outward and one inward. What each feels like, and what helps during one.

Adults are not supposed to have meltdowns. That is the message most autistic adults absorbed early, which is why a meltdown at forty tends to arrive with a second wave of shame on top of the first.

They happen anyway. So do shutdowns, which are quieter and much more often missed. Both are involuntary responses to overload, and understanding them takes a good deal of the shame out.

Key takeaways

  • A meltdown is an outward loss of control in response to overwhelm. A shutdown is an inward one.
  • Neither is chosen, and neither is a tantrum aimed at getting something.
  • Both usually result from accumulated load, so the final trigger is rarely the real cause.
  • During either one, the most helpful thing is less: less input, fewer questions, more space.
  • Learning your early warning signs is the most useful prevention there is.

What a meltdown is

A meltdown happens when overwhelm exceeds the point where you can regulate your responses. Control of your reactions goes, temporarily.

It can look like crying that will not stop, shouting, intense agitation, pacing, hitting objects, or a need to escape immediately. Some people describe it from inside as a kind of storm, where they can sometimes observe what is happening and cannot stop it.

The comparison to a tantrum comes up constantly and is worth answering directly, because most people believe it. A tantrum is aimed at an outcome, and it often stops when the outcome is achieved or when there is no audience. A meltdown is not aimed at anything. Giving the person what they seem to want does not end it, and it happens just as much alone.

What a shutdown is

A shutdown is the same overload turned inward. Instead of exploding, the system goes offline.

You become less responsive, sometimes almost unresponsive. Speech can become very difficult or stop altogether. Thinking slows. Movement becomes hard. You might sit or lie very still, staring, unable to answer simple questions or make the smallest decision.

Shutdowns are missed constantly, because they are quiet. From outside they can look like sulking, rudeness or being withdrawn. People get criticised for not replying or for "going silent", when what was happening was a system protecting itself from more input.

Many people, particularly those who learned early that outward reactions were unacceptable, have far more shutdowns than meltdowns.

A meltdown is the system overflowing. A shutdown is the system switching itself off. Both are protection.

What causes them

Usually not the thing that seems to cause them.

The visible trigger is often minor: a plan changing, a noise, a question at the wrong moment, a dropped glass. Underneath it is a load that has been accumulating for hours or days. Sensory input, social effort, masking, uncertainty, hunger, tiredness, changes in routine. Each one adds to the total. The trigger is just the moment the total crosses the line.

That is why the same event can be completely fine one day and the final straw the next. The event did not change. Your remaining capacity did.

Being in autistic burnout lowers the threshold considerably, so meltdowns and shutdowns often become more frequent during burnout, which can be frightening if you do not know why.

Warning signs

Most people have signs before the line is crossed, even if they have learned to ignore them.

Common ones include rising irritability, sounds or lights becoming more intrusive, losing track of conversations, stimming becoming more intense, a strong urge to leave, clumsiness, difficulty finding words, and a sense of pressure building in the head or chest.

Interoception and alexithymia, difficulty sensing your internal state and naming emotions, can make these harder to catch. A practical way round it is to check in on a schedule rather than waiting to notice. "How loud does everything feel, out of ten?" at a few fixed times is cruder than intuition and more reliable.

What helps during one

Less of everything. Fewer words, less light, less noise, fewer people.

If you are the one experiencing it, the priority is getting somewhere with less input, if you can. Headphones, a quiet room, a car, a bathroom. Leaving is allowed. Explaining can come later.

If you are supporting someone, do not ask lots of questions, do not try to reason with them, and do not touch them unless you know they want that. Say very little, calmly. Reduce the input around them. Make sure they are safe and stay nearby if that helps, at a distance if it does not. Wait.

For shutdowns in particular, do not demand answers. A shutdown often removes the ability to speak, and pressure to respond adds load.

Afterwards

Recovery takes longer than the event. Exhaustion, a headache, emotional flatness or a need for a lot of sleep afterwards are common.

It is better not to dissect what happened straight away. Analysis is another demand, and you have nothing left. Once you have recovered, it can be useful to look back at what built up beforehand, not to assign blame, but to find the load you can reduce next time.

And try to drop the shame where you can. A meltdown or shutdown is a nervous system reaching its limit. It says something about the load, and nothing about your character.

Getting support

If meltdowns or shutdowns are frequent, are getting worse, or involve any risk of harm to yourself or others, it is worth talking to a professional, ideally one who understands autistic adults. If you are ever in immediate danger, contact emergency services.

Common questions

What is the difference between a meltdown and a shutdown?

Both are involuntary responses to overwhelm. A meltdown is outward: crying, shouting, agitation or a need to escape. A shutdown is inward: withdrawing, going quiet, and sometimes losing the ability to speak or move easily. The same person can have both.

Do autistic adults have meltdowns?

Yes. Meltdowns do not stop in adulthood, though many adults have learned to hold them in until they are alone, or experience shutdowns more often instead. Adult meltdowns are common and are frequently surrounded by a lot of shame.

Is a meltdown the same as a tantrum?

No. A tantrum is aimed at an outcome and often stops when it is achieved or when there is no audience. A meltdown is an involuntary loss of control caused by overload. It is not aimed at anything, and it happens whether or not anyone is watching.

How can I help someone having a meltdown?

Reduce the input around them, speak very little and calmly, avoid touching them unless you know they want it, and make sure they are safe. Do not try to reason with them or ask a series of questions. Give them time and space to come out of it.

Can meltdowns be prevented?

Not always, but they can often be made less frequent by reducing the overall load and by learning your early warning signs. The Late-Diagnosed Autistic Workbook covers reading your energy and sensory load before it reaches zero.
