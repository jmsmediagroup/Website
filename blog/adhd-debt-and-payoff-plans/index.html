ADHD and debt: why payoff plans keep failing, and what to do instead

Debt payoff plans assume consistency, which ADHD does not reliably supply. Why the usual methods fail, and how to build one that survives a bad month.

By J.M. Struijk · 21 October 2026 · 7 min read
Filed underADHD and money

Most debt advice is arithmetic, and the arithmetic is not wrong. List your debts, attack them in a sensible order, pay more than the minimum, repeat until finished.

The arithmetic has never been the part I failed at. I could explain the optimal method confidently while not opening a letter for six weeks.

If your debt has survived several serious attempts to clear it, the problem is probably not that you chose the wrong method.

Key takeaways

  • Standard payoff plans assume steady monthly action, which ADHD supplies unevenly.
  • Avoidance is the most expensive single factor, and it grows the longer a thing goes unopened.
  • Optimal plans are fragile, and a fragile plan that gets abandoned beats no plan only slightly.
  • Automation beats intention, because it survives the weeks when you have nothing.
  • One early visible win matters more than mathematical efficiency, because momentum is the scarce resource.

Why the standard methods break

The two popular approaches are to pay the highest interest first, or to pay the smallest balance first for the psychological lift.

Both are fine on paper. Both assume you will look at your debts every month, decide an amount, make a payment, and keep doing that for two or three years without the system falling over.

That assumption is the whole problem. ADHD does not produce a flat line of effort. It produces bursts and troughs. In a burst you can do a year's worth of financial admin in one evening. In a trough you cannot open an envelope. A plan that needs a constant monthly input is designed for a kind of consistency that is not available, and the first missed month usually ends it, because a missed month is evidence of failure and evidence of failure produces avoidance.

The plans are not badly designed. They are designed for a different machine.

Avoidance is the expensive part

If I had to name the single thing that has cost me most money, it is not interest rates. It is unopened post.

The mechanism is familiar to anyone who has lived it. A letter arrives that is probably bad. Opening it would produce a feeling you do not have the capacity for today, so it goes on a pile. The next day it is slightly worse, because now it is both the original problem and evidence that you avoided it. The pile gets harder to approach in proportion to how long it has been there, and the thing inside is quietly accruing fees the whole time.

The cruel detail is that the contents are almost always less bad than the pile suggests. Most creditors would rather arrange something than escalate. The fees that accumulate during avoidance frequently exceed what the original problem would have cost to resolve.

Knowing this does not dissolve the avoidance, because avoidance is not an information deficit. What helps is reducing what opening the thing requires: doing it with another person in the room, doing all of it in one sitting rather than one letter at a time, or having a fixed slot where the task has a beginning and an end.

The pile is almost always worse than its contents. The cost is in the waiting, not in the letter.

Build a plan that survives a bad month

The design goal is different from the usual one. You are not optimising for the fastest payoff under ideal conditions. You are optimising for a plan that is still running in eight months, including the months where you are not functioning well.

Automate the minimums first, before anything else. Every debt, automatic payment, minimum amount, from an account that will cover it. This is the floor. It means a bad month costs you progress but does not cost you fees, a default, or a mark on your credit file. The floor matters more than the ceiling, because the floor is what holds when you are not there.

Then make extra payments manual and opportunistic. Counterintuitive, but the bursts are real and worth using. When a good week arrives, throw money at the target debt. Do not commit in advance to an aggressive monthly figure that assumes every month is a good one. Committing to the maximum means failing most months, and failing most months ends plans.

Pick one target debt at a time, and pick the one that will clear soonest. Mathematically this is usually not optimal. It is still what I would recommend, because closing an account produces a visible, final result, and visible results are the fuel that keeps the thing going. An approach that is five percent more efficient and gets abandoned in month four loses to a slightly worse approach that survives.

Keep a single sheet showing every debt, the balance, and the minimum, and put it somewhere you will physically see it. Not a spreadsheet you have to open. A piece of paper. Out of sight genuinely does mean out of mind here, and most of the avoidance happens in the gap between knowing a thing exists and being able to look at it.

Finally, give yourself a defined review slot, once a month, with a time limit. Twenty minutes, diarised. The limit matters as much as the slot, because an open-ended financial review is exactly the kind of task that gets postponed forever.

Talk to the people you owe

This is the piece most people skip, and it tends to produce the largest single improvement.

Creditors have hardship processes. Interest can sometimes be frozen, payments reduced, plans arranged. None of that is advertised prominently, and all of it generally requires you to ask.

Making that call is unpleasant. It involves admitting to a stranger that you are struggling, usually several times, usually on hold. I have found it helps to write down what to say first, because the conversation tends to produce the kind of shame that makes it hard to think, and reading a prepared sentence is easier than composing one.

If the debt is substantial, a free debt advice service is worth more than anything I can write here. They negotiate for a living, they know what each creditor will actually agree to, and they do not charge. Avoid anyone who wants a fee to manage your debt.

The shame loop again

Debt and shame are wound together tightly enough that it is hard to work on one without the other.

The loop is predictable. Avoiding the debt makes it worse. The debt getting worse produces more shame. More shame produces more avoidance. The person inside that loop looks, from outside, like someone who does not care, when in fact the loop runs on caring too much to look.

The way out that worked for me was a plain one. Debt is a number, arrived at through a combination of circumstances, choices and an undiagnosed condition that made a particular set of mistakes very likely. It is not a verdict. Treating it as a verdict is what keeps the envelope closed, and the envelope staying closed is what makes it grow.

Common questions

Should I use the snowball or avalanche method with ADHD?

Smallest balance first, in most cases. The avalanche method saves more in interest, but it can be a long time before anything visibly completes, and sustained invisible progress is hard for an ADHD brain to stay attached to. Clearing an account entirely gives you a result you can see, and that tends to keep the plan alive.

What if I cannot even look at my debts?

Start with the total rather than the details. One session, one page, every balance written down with nothing else required of you. No plan, no calls, no decisions. Knowing the real number is usually less frightening than the imagined one, and the imagined one is what avoidance feeds on.

Does ADHD make debt more likely?

It affects the systems that keep debt from accumulating: paying on time, tracking balances, resisting impulse purchases, opening post, planning ahead. None of that is destiny, and plenty of people with ADHD carry no debt at all. It does mean that when debt appears, it often has more to do with executive function than with income.

Will a debt management plan hurt my credit?

It can, and the specifics depend on the type of arrangement and where you live. A free debt advice service will explain the trade-offs for your situation, which is exactly the sort of question worth asking someone qualified rather than reading about. Missed payments and defaults also affect credit, so doing nothing is rarely the neutral option it feels like.

Where should I start today?

Set up automatic minimum payments on everything. One afternoon, and it removes the most expensive failure mode permanently. The ADHD Debt Payoff Workbook walks through the rest of this process step by step if you want the structured version.
