When your child's assessment starts describing you

A great many adults first recognise their own ADHD or autism while sitting in their child's assessment. Why it happens, and what to do with it.

By J.M. Struijk · 24 October 2026 · 7 min read
Filed underLate diagnosis: ADHD and autism in adulthood

You are in a room answering questions about your child. Does he line things up. Does he struggle when plans change. Does he talk at length about one subject without noticing whether anyone is still listening.

And somewhere around the fourth question you stop writing, because you have just realised you would answer yes to most of them about yourself, and that you had always assumed everybody would.

This happens constantly. It is one of the most common routes to adult identification there is, and almost nobody is warned about it before they walk into the room.

Key takeaways

  • Both ADHD and autism run strongly in families, so a child's assessment often raises questions about a parent.
  • The recognition usually arrives through the questionnaire rather than through any conversation about you.
  • Having coped does not mean it was not there. It often means it was expensive.
  • You do not need your own diagnosis to use what you have just learned.
  • The emotional part, which tends to include anger about your own childhood, is worth expecting.

Why it happens so often

Neither of these conditions appears from nowhere. ADHD and autism are both strongly heritable, which is unremarkable to clinicians and news to most parents. When a child is identified, the chance that a parent shares something is simply not small.

What makes the assessment the moment of recognition, rather than any of the forty years preceding it, is the questionnaire. Assessment forms ask unusually specific questions. Not "is your child sociable" but whether he finds eye contact uncomfortable, whether he takes instructions literally, whether a change to the route home ruins the afternoon.

Those are the questions nobody had ever asked you either. General life does not interrogate you about whether you need to know the plan in advance. A clinical form does, about your child, and you answer it with your own experience sitting right there in the room.

Nobody had ever asked you the question in a form specific enough to answer honestly. Then someone asked it about your child.

You calibrated normal on yourself

This is the part that explains the delay.

Everyone's baseline for ordinary is their own experience. If you have always needed twenty minutes alone after a social event, that is not a trait you notice, it is simply what evenings are. If you have never been able to start a task without an external deadline, you are not aware of a difficulty, you have a personality.

So when your child does the same things, two readings are available. Either this is a difficulty requiring support, or this is just what people are like, because it is what you are like.

Parents in this position frequently under-report. Asked whether their child struggles with something, they say no, genuinely, because the child is doing roughly what the parent did at that age and the parent came out the other side. The honest answer is often yes, and the reason it reads as no is that the measuring instrument has the same bias as the thing being measured.

"But I coped"

This is the sentence that stops most people from going further, and it deserves looking at directly.

What parents often sayWhat is usually underneath it
"I managed fine at school"You managed. Whether it was fine is a separate question, and the cost was invisible to everyone including you.
"It can't be the same thing, he struggles much more"Support needs vary enormously between people with the same thing, and children are not permitted the workarounds adults build.
"I'd have been picked up as a child"Identification depended heavily on when and where you grew up, and on whether you were disruptive. Quiet children were not referred.
"I've got a job and a family, so clearly I'm fine"Plenty of identified adults have both. Function and difficulty are not opposites.
"I don't want to take attention from him"Nothing about understanding yourself reduces what is available to your child. It usually increases it.

The common thread is the assumption that coping disproves the thing. It does not. Masking and compensation are how a great many people reach adulthood unidentified, and the reason they are worth knowing about is that both carry a running cost you have been paying without an itemised bill.

The feelings arrive in an unhelpful order

People expect relief. Relief does turn up, and it is real.

What surprises people is the anger, and specifically that it is retrospective. You are not angry about now. You are angry on behalf of a child who was called lazy, or difficult, or too sensitive, and who is you, and who nobody helped. That can sit very strangely alongside gratitude that your own child is being helped at eight rather than at forty-five.

There is often grief underneath it about the version of your life that might have happened with support. The first year after an adult diagnosis covers that ground, because relief and grief arriving together is the norm rather than a complication.

And there can be guilt, which is worth dismantling quickly. Passing on a heritable trait is not something a person does. You did not choose your genes and you did not administer them. If what you passed on also came with a parent who understands the inside of it, your child is better placed than most.

What to do with it

You have three options and they are all reasonable.

Pursue your own assessment. The practical reasons are workplace adjustments, access to support, and, for some people, treatment options to discuss with a doctor. How to get assessed as an adult covers the route, and whether this counts as a disability covers what protections may follow. It costs time and often money, and waiting lists are long.

Act on it without a diagnosis. Nothing stops you using what you now know. Noise-cancelling headphones do not check your paperwork. Neither does writing things down, or protecting an hour of recovery after a demanding day. Many people stop here and are entirely content.

Sit with it. Deciding not to decide is a legitimate position, particularly while your child's support is being set up and your attention is reasonably elsewhere. The question keeps.

What tends not to work is treating it as settled because of an online test. Those tests are screening instruments at best, and the good ones say so themselves.

The part that helps your child

Here is the argument for taking it seriously, if the ones about you are not persuasive.

You can translate. When your child says a jumper is unbearable, you are not deciding whether to believe him, because you know what that feels like from inside. When a school suggests the behaviour is attention-seeking, you have grounds to push back that a parent without this experience does not have.

You will also catch the masking, which is the thing professionals miss most often. A child who presents well in a one-hour appointment and falls apart at home is doing something you probably recognise. Being able to name that in a meeting changes what happens in the meeting.

Parenting a child who shares your wiring has a harder side too, and it is worth saying plainly rather than skipping to the redemptive part. Your sensory limits and his will collide. The noise he needs to make is sometimes exactly the noise you cannot tolerate, and that collision arrives at the end of the day when neither of you has anything left. Knowing why it happens does not stop it happening. It does stop you concluding that one of you is the problem.

Common questions

Can a child's autism diagnosis mean the parent is autistic too?

It raises the possibility, because autism runs strongly in families, but it settles nothing on its own. Many parents of autistic children are not autistic. What a child's assessment often does is prompt a parent to look at their own experience with questions specific enough to be answerable for the first time.

Is ADHD inherited from the mother or the father?

Neither specifically. ADHD is strongly heritable and can come through either parent, and it is not a single-gene trait that follows a simple inheritance pattern. It is also common for one parent to be identified only after a child is.

Why do so many parents realise during their child's assessment?

Assessment questionnaires ask very specific questions that ordinary life never asks. A parent answering them about their child is reading, often for the first time, a precise description of things they assumed were universal.

Should I get assessed if my child has been diagnosed?

That depends on what you want from it. A diagnosis can open workplace adjustments, formal support and treatment conversations with a doctor. If you mainly want to understand yourself and adjust how you live, you can do a good deal of that without one. Neither choice is wrong.

Will my own diagnosis affect my child's support?

No. Your child's support is assessed on your child. Some parents worry that being identified themselves will cause their reports to be taken less seriously, and while that fear is understandable, understanding the condition from inside tends to make a parent a better advocate rather than a less credible one.

I coped without support, so is it really the same thing?

Coping does not rule it out. Support needs vary widely between people, adults have built workarounds that children are not allowed, and quiet children were historically not referred at all. The question is not whether you survived but what it cost you, which is usually invisible until someone asks.
