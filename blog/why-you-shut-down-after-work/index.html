Why you shut down after work, and what helps

Home from work and unable to move, talk or start anything. Why ADHD and autistic adults hit a wall after the working day, and what makes the evening easier.

You get home, sit down for a minute in your coat, and an hour later you are still there. The phone is in your hand, unread. You meant to eat, reply to a message, put a wash on. Nothing has happened, and the part of you that would normally make it happen is not answering.

Then someone asks what you want for dinner and the question feels physically impossible to answer.

This is extremely common among ADHD and autistic adults, and the explanation is fairly ordinary: the working day cost a great deal more than it appeared to.

Key takeaways

  • The collapse usually reflects how much the working day cost, not how hard the evening is.
  • Masking, sensory load and constant decisions all draw on the same limited capacity.
  • It tends to arrive at home because home is where it is finally safe to stop.
  • ADHD paralysis, autistic shutdown and ordinary tiredness feel similar but respond to different things.
  • A short, protected gap between work and the rest of the evening helps more than pushing through.

The cost of the day arrives all at once

A working day asks for a great deal that never appears on a job description.

There is the masking: monitoring your face and tone, judging when to speak, laughing at the right moment, suppressing the movement that helps you think. There is the sensory load of an office, a shop floor or a commute. And there are the decisions, hundreds of them, each small and each drawing on the same executive function you would need later to cook a meal or answer a text.

None of that feels dramatic while it is happening. You are coping, and coping looks like nothing from outside. The bill comes when the demands stop. The moment you are somewhere you no longer have to hold it together, the system that has been holding it together goes quiet.

There is a name for this, borrowed from parents: restraint collapse. It was first used about children who are perfectly composed at school and fall apart within minutes of getting home. The pattern is the same in adults. The composure was real, and it was expensive, and home is where the payment is collected.

Getting through the day and the day costing you nothing are two different things. The evening is where the difference shows up.

Paralysis, shutdown or just tired

These overlap and plenty of people get all three, but they are worth telling apart, because what helps differs.

What it feels likeWhat usually sets it offWhat tends to help
ADHD paralysisWanting to do things and being unable to start any of themToo many open tasks and decisions, nothing clearly firstOne small, specific task chosen in advance, or someone else in the room
Autistic shutdownWords get hard, processing slows, you need to be left aloneSensory and social overload building through the dayLow light, quiet, no questions, and time
Ordinary tirednessYou want to rest, and resting worksA long or physically hard dayFood, sleep, an early night

Why it happens at home and not at work

People are sometimes told, or tell themselves, that if they can manage at work they ought to manage at home. It runs the other way.

Holding it together takes effort that only feels sustainable while something is enforcing it: a manager, a deadline, colleagues who would notice. Home removes the enforcement. That is exactly what makes it safe to stop, and exactly why the stopping happens there and not at three in the afternoon.

For autistic adults the masking is often the largest single cost, and it is invisible to everyone, including sometimes the person doing it. For ADHD adults it is more often the sheer number of decisions and interruptions. Most people who identify as both recognise both.

The first hour after you walk in

The evening tends to go better if the first stretch of it asks nothing of you. A few things that make that more likely:

Build in a gap before anything else is expected. Twenty or thirty minutes with no conversation, no decisions and no screen that demands replies. Sitting in the car, a walk round the block, a shower, lying on the floor. It looks like wasted time and it usually saves the rest of the evening.

Tell the people you live with what is going on. "I need half an hour before I can talk" lands very differently from silence, and stops the shutdown from being read as coldness.

Take the decisions out of the evening in advance. Dinner decided in the morning, or a fallback meal that needs no thought. Clothes for tomorrow laid out at the weekend. Every choice made earlier is one you do not have to make with nothing left.

Pick one thing. If something has to happen tonight, choose it before you leave work and keep it small and concrete, like putting the bins out. A list of five things at seven in the evening is usually where paralysis starts.

Lower the input on the way home. Headphones, the quieter route, a different carriage. Some of the overload happens on the commute itself.

Reducing what the day takes

The first hour helps you recover. Making the day itself cheaper means there is less to recover from, and that is where the bigger change usually sits.

That might mean fewer meetings back to back, a quieter desk, written instructions in place of verbal ones, or permission to work with headphones on. Many of these are ordinary requests that a reasonable employer will agree to, and they are covered in ADHD at work and autistic at work. It can also mean masking a little less where it is safe to, which is easier said than done and still worth working towards.

When it is more than a hard evening

A flat evening after a demanding day is normal. Every evening being like that is different.

The warning signs are mostly about recovery. If weekends no longer restore you, if a holiday helps for a few days and then the flatness returns, if things you used to manage without thinking are becoming hard, that points towards burnout rather than tiredness. Burnout does not respond to rest alone, because what needs to change is the load.

If that sounds familiar, or if low mood is creeping in alongside it, it is worth speaking to a doctor. It is also worth looking hard at what the working week is costing, because that is usually the part that has to change.

Common questions

Why do I shut down after work?

Usually because the working day used up more capacity than it appeared to. Masking, sensory load and constant small decisions all draw on the same limited resource, and the effects tend to arrive once the demands stop, which is when you get home. It is very common among ADHD and autistic adults.

Why can't I move or start anything after work?

For many ADHD adults this is task paralysis: wanting to do things but being unable to begin, often because too many tasks are waiting and none is clearly first. Choosing one small, specific task before you leave work, and keeping the first part of the evening free of demands, tends to help more than willpower.

What is an autistic shutdown after work?

A state where processing slows, speech gets difficult and you need to withdraw, usually after sensory and social overload has built up through the day. It is a protective response rather than a choice, and it eases with quiet, low light and time.

What is restraint collapse in adults?

A term first used for children who stay composed at school and fall apart at home. Adults experience the same thing: holding it together through the working day, then losing the ability to function once they are somewhere safe enough to stop.

Is it normal to be too tired to do anything after work?

Occasionally, after a hard day, yes. If it happens every evening and weekends no longer restore you, that suggests the load is too high or that burnout is developing, and it is worth taking seriously.

How do I stop shutting down after work?

Two things help most. Protect the first twenty to thirty minutes at home from any demands, and make the working day itself cheaper through adjustments, fewer back-to-back demands and less masking where it is safe. Recovery strategies help, but reducing the load is what changes the pattern.

Is shutting down after work a sign of burnout?

Not on its own. It becomes a concern when it is constant, when rest and weekends stop helping, and when everyday skills start to slip. That pattern is worth discussing with a doctor.
