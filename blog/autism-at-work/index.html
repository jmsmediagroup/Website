Autistic at work: the hidden costs, and the adjustments that help

Why autistic adults often find work exhausting even when they are good at it, what the real costs are, and the adjustments and conversations that help.

By J.M. Struijk · 21 October 2026 · 5 min read
Filed underAutism in adults

Plenty of autistic adults are very good at their jobs. Thorough, accurate, honest, deeply knowledgeable about the things they work on. And plenty of them are exhausted by work in a way that has very little to do with the work itself.

The tasks are often the easy part. The hard part is everything around them: the office, the meetings, the small talk, the unwritten rules, the constant performance of being an easy colleague.

Key takeaways

  • For many autistic adults, the cost of work comes from the environment and the social layer, not the tasks.
  • Masking at work is common, effective and expensive, and it is a frequent route into burnout.
  • Sensory adjustments and clear communication are usually the most valuable and the cheapest to provide.
  • Recruitment processes often test social performance rather than ability, which filters out capable autistic people.
  • Legal rights to adjustments exist in many countries, and you can ask for specific changes without disclosing.

Where the cost actually comes from

Sensory load is usually first. Open-plan offices combine noise, overhead lighting, movement in your peripheral vision, strong smells from the kitchen and someone else's phone conversation. You can be completely capable of the work and still spend most of your energy filtering the room.

The social layer is next. Small talk in the kitchen, lunch with the team, knowing when a joke is a joke, reading what your manager meant rather than what they said. Each interaction requires translation, and translation is effort.

Then the unwritten rules. Many workplaces run on expectations nobody states: how quickly to reply to messages, how direct you can be, who actually decides things. Autistic people often follow the written rules exactly and get caught out by the unwritten ones.

Uncertainty adds to all of it. Last-minute meeting changes, vague instructions, shifting priorities. Each one costs more than it seems to from outside.

And masking runs underneath everything. Monitoring your face, rehearsing what to say, suppressing movement you would normally use to regulate. It works, which is why people do it, and it is exhausting, which is why so many autistic adults reach burnout through work.

The work might take six hours. Being seen to fit in while doing it takes the rest of the day.

Adjustments that help

The most useful changes tend to be cheap and specific.

Sensory adjustments come first. A quieter desk away from the kitchen and the walkways, permission to wear noise-cancelling headphones, softer lighting or a desk lamp instead of overhead fluorescents, the option to work from home on demanding days.

Clear, written communication. Instructions in writing, agendas before meetings, decisions confirmed by email afterwards. This removes guesswork and gives you something to refer back to.

Notice of change. Knowing about schedule changes in advance, even by a day, makes a large difference to how much they cost.

Explicit expectations. What does good work look like in this role? How fast should messages be answered? What counts as urgent? Unwritten rules made written.

Flexibility around social events. Team lunches and away-days can be optional, or attended briefly, without it being treated as a lack of commitment.

A named person to ask. Someone you can check things with directly, rather than having to decode what is expected.

Interviews

Recruitment is often where autistic people are filtered out before ability is ever tested. Traditional interviews reward eye contact, confident small talk and quick answers to hypothetical questions, none of which predict whether someone can do the job.

Things that help: asking for the questions in advance, a work sample or practical task instead of, or alongside, the interview, extra time, or a written format. Many employers will agree if asked, and some now offer these routinely.

Your rights, briefly

I am not a lawyer, and this varies by country, so use it as a starting point. In the United States, the Americans with Disabilities Act can require reasonable accommodations, with employment protections applying to employers of 15 or more people, and the Job Accommodation Network gives free, confidential guidance. In the United Kingdom, the Equality Act 2010 sets out when employers must make reasonable adjustments, and Access to Work can fund support in England, Scotland and Wales. The resources directory links to the official sources.

Telling your employer

Disclosing can make adjustments easier to get and can give legal protection. It can also change how you are treated, and workplaces vary enormously in how well they handle it.

You can ask for many adjustments without disclosing anything. "I concentrate much better somewhere quieter; could I move to the desk by the window?" is a reasonable request on its own terms. If you need formal adjustments or protection later, you can disclose then.

Common questions

Should I tell my employer I am autistic?

That depends on your workplace, your legal protections and what you need. Disclosure can make formal adjustments easier to get, while asking for specific changes without disclosing works well in many places. It is your decision, and you can change it later.

What reasonable adjustments help autistic employees?

Common examples include a quieter workspace, noise-cancelling headphones, adjusted lighting, written instructions and agendas, advance notice of changes, clearly stated expectations, flexible hours or remote work, and optional social events. The Job Accommodation Network lists more ideas.

Why is work so exhausting when I am good at my job?

For many autistic adults, the effort goes into the environment and the social layer: filtering sensory input, translating communication, following unwritten rules and masking. That load continues all day regardless of how easy the tasks are, and it is a common route into burnout.

How can I make job interviews easier?

Ask for the questions in advance, a practical task or work sample, extra time, or a written format. Many employers will agree to these if asked. Explaining that you communicate best in writing is a reasonable request without needing to disclose a diagnosis.

Where can I find out about my rights?

The resources directory lists official and expert sources, including the Job Accommodation Network, Acas and Access to Work. The Late-Diagnosed Autistic Workbook includes self-advocacy scripts and a section on accommodations.
