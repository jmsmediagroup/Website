Online ADHD and autism tests: what they can and cannot tell you

Online ADHD and autism self-tests are screeners, not diagnoses. What a high or low score means, why results mislead, and how to use them well.

By J.M. Struijk · 21 October 2026 · 4 min read
Filed underLate diagnosis: ADHD and autism in adulthood

Most people who suspect they are ADHD or autistic take an online test at some point, usually late at night, usually more than once. Then they sit with a score and try to work out what it means.

Online tests can be genuinely useful. They are also easy to misread, in both directions. This is an honest guide to what they are, what they measure, and how to use the result.

Key takeaways

  • Online ADHD and autism tests are screening questionnaires, and none of them can diagnose you.
  • A high score suggests an assessment may be worth pursuing. It does not confirm anything.
  • Anxiety, depression, trauma and burnout can raise scores, and masking can lower them.
  • A low score does not rule anything out, especially if you have spent years compensating.
  • The most useful thing a test gives you is language for a conversation with a clinician.

Screening is not diagnosis

The questionnaires most often found online are screening tools. They were designed to flag people who might benefit from a proper assessment, not to make a diagnosis on their own.

For ADHD, the most widely used adult screener is the World Health Organization's Adult ADHD Self-Report Scale, usually shortened to ASRS. ADDA offers a free online version and states plainly that it is a screening tool rather than a diagnosis.

For autism, common questionnaires include the AQ, or Autism-Spectrum Quotient, the RAADS-R, and the CAT-Q, which measures camouflaging rather than autism itself. Embrace Autism hosts free versions of these and others. Links to both sites are in the resources directory.

A diagnosis comes from a clinician, through a detailed interview, developmental history and clinical judgement, ideally considering other explanations. A questionnaire is one small input into that process at most.

What a high score means

A high score means your answers resemble those of people who were later diagnosed. That is worth taking seriously, and it may be a good reason to seek an assessment.

It is not confirmation, because several other things can produce similar answers.

Anxiety can look like inattention, restlessness and difficulty concentrating. Depression can look like poor motivation and executive dysfunction. Trauma can produce hypervigilance, dissociation and difficulty with focus. Burnout can make almost any executive function questionnaire look severe. Sleep deprivation affects attention dramatically.

None of that means your experience is not real. It means the cause needs working out by someone who can consider all the options.

What a low score means

A low score is easier to over-trust, and it can do more harm.

Many late-identified adults have spent decades compensating. They answer questions about how they behave, and their behaviour looks fine, because they have built it carefully. Questions like "do you find it hard to make eye contact?" can be answered "no" by someone who forces eye contact through deliberate effort every day.

If you score low but strongly recognise yourself in descriptions of ADHD or autism, or if a test measuring camouflaging suggests you mask heavily, a low score on a trait questionnaire should not end the question.

A low score from someone who has spent thirty years compensating is not the reassurance it looks like.

How to use a result well

Treat it as a conversation starter. Bring your score and, more importantly, the specific questions you answered strongly, to a clinician. "I scored high on the screener, and these items in particular describe my life" is a good way to begin.

Pay attention to which items resonate, more than the total. The items you recognised instantly are often more informative than the number.

Take tests on a reasonably ordinary day. Answering in the middle of a crisis or a burnout can inflate scores in ways that do not reflect your baseline.

Do not take dozens. Repeating tests until you get the answer you are hoping for, in either direction, tends to produce anxiety rather than clarity.

Common questions

Are online ADHD tests accurate?

They are screening tools, designed to identify people who might benefit from assessment, not to diagnose. A high score suggests an assessment could be worthwhile, but anxiety, depression, trauma, burnout and poor sleep can all raise scores. Only a clinician can diagnose ADHD.

Can an online test tell me if I am autistic?

No. Online autism questionnaires such as the AQ and RAADS-R are screening tools. They can suggest whether an assessment may be worth pursuing, but diagnosis requires a clinical assessment. Masking can also lower scores for people who are autistic.

What is the ASRS?

The Adult ADHD Self-Report Scale is a screening questionnaire developed with the World Health Organization. It is widely used as a first step to identify adults who might benefit from a full ADHD assessment. It does not provide a diagnosis.

What does a low score on an autism test mean?

It means your answers did not closely resemble those of diagnosed autistic people on that questionnaire. For people who mask heavily, trait questionnaires can underestimate autistic traits, so a low score does not necessarily rule autism out if you strongly recognise yourself in autistic accounts.

What should I do after a high score?

Note which questions resonated most, write down concrete examples from your life, and talk to a doctor about assessment. The article on how to get assessed as an adult covers the process and how to prepare.
