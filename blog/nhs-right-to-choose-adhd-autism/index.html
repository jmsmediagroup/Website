NHS Right to Choose for ADHD and autism assessment

In England you can usually choose who does your NHS ADHD or autism assessment, which can cut a long wait. How it works, and what it does not cover.

If you are in England and your local wait for an ADHD or autism assessment is measured in years, there is a route most people are never told about. You can usually choose a different provider, and the wait with that provider may be far shorter.

It is not a loophole or a trick. It is an ordinary part of how NHS referrals work, and the NHS says so plainly: when a GP refers you, in most cases you have the legal right to choose the hospital or service you would like to go to.

Two things to be clear about before anything else. This applies in England only. And this is a description of a referral process, not medical or legal advice; the official sources are linked at the end.

Key takeaways

  • In England you can usually choose which provider does your NHS assessment, at the point of referral.
  • The provider has to hold an NHS contract for that service, and the referral has to be clinically appropriate.
  • It does not apply in Scotland, Wales or Northern Ireland, which have their own systems.
  • It does not apply to urgent or emergency care, or if you are already being seen for the same thing.
  • Ask what happens after diagnosis, because providers differ on whether they offer ongoing care.

What the right actually is

When your GP refers you for a first appointment for a new problem, you can normally choose which provider you go to, rather than being sent automatically to whoever covers your postcode. The NHS describes telling your GP your preference during the appointment, after which the GP puts a shortlist of providers on your referral and you book through the NHS e-Referral service.

The choice is not unlimited. The provider must hold an NHS contract to deliver that service, and the referral has to be clinically appropriate for what you need. You cannot choose a kind of service that does not suit the problem.

Where this matters for ADHD and autism is simple arithmetic. Local assessment waits in many areas are extremely long. Some NHS-contracted providers operating across England have much shorter ones, and using this right costs you nothing, because it is still NHS care.

If you live in Scotland, Wales or Northern Ireland, none of this is available to you, and I would rather say that plainly than have you spend an afternoon on it.

How to actually do it

Work out which provider you want first. Look at providers that hold NHS contracts for ADHD or autism assessment, and check their current waiting time, which they usually publish. Check whether they assess adults, since not all do.

Then ask your GP for a referral to that provider specifically, and say you are exercising your right to choose. Some GPs do this routinely. Others have not come across it, which is common and not a refusal. Several charities publish template letters you can bring to the appointment, which makes the conversation much easier than explaining it from memory.

Expect to provide the same background a local referral would need: how the difficulties affect you now, and what you can recall of childhood. The article on getting assessed as an adult covers preparing for that.

The question to ask before you commit

What happens after the assessment?

This is the part people most often discover too late. Some providers assess and then discharge you back to your GP. If you are diagnosed and want medication, your GP has to agree to take over prescribing, usually under a shared care agreement, and GPs are not obliged to enter one.

ADHD UK's guidance suggests raising shared care with your GP before the assessment rather than after, which strikes me as the single most useful thing on this page. A short conversation beforehand can prevent a diagnosis that you then cannot act on.

A shorter wait for the assessment is worth little if nobody will prescribe afterwards. Ask first.

If your GP says no

A refusal is not always the end of it. Sometimes it reflects unfamiliarity rather than policy, and bringing a template letter or the NHS guidance is enough.

If you believe you were not offered a choice you were entitled to, the NHS route is to raise it with the referring practice first, then with your integrated care board, and after that with the Parliamentary and Health Service Ombudsman.

It is also worth knowing that a GP can decline if the referral would not be clinically appropriate, which is a legitimate clinical judgement rather than an obstruction.

Common questions

What is NHS Right to Choose?

It is the right, in England, to choose which provider carries out your first NHS appointment for a new problem, rather than being referred automatically to your local service. For ADHD and autism it is commonly used to avoid very long local assessment waits. The provider must hold an NHS contract and the referral must be clinically appropriate.

Does Right to Choose apply in Scotland, Wales or Northern Ireland?

No. It is an England arrangement. The other UK nations run their own referral systems, and this particular right does not exist there in the same form.

Is Right to Choose free?

Yes. It is NHS care delivered by a provider holding an NHS contract, so there is no charge to you. That is the main difference from going private.

Can my GP refuse a Right to Choose referral?

A GP can decline if the referral would not be clinically appropriate. Unfamiliarity with the process is more common than outright refusal, and bringing the NHS guidance or a template letter often resolves it. If you think you were wrongly denied a choice you were entitled to, raise it with the practice, then your integrated care board.

What should I check before choosing a provider?

Their current waiting time, whether they assess adults, and above all what happens after diagnosis. If you might want medication, ask your GP about a shared care agreement before the assessment rather than after.
