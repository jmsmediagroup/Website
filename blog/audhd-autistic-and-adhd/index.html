AuDHD: what it is like being autistic and ADHD at the same time

AuDHD means being both autistic and ADHD. The two sets of needs pull against each other, which is why advice for either one alone tends to fail.

By J.M. Struijk · 21 October 2026 · 6 min read

For a long time these two were not allowed to coexist. Diagnostic rules in several countries actively prevented clinicians from recording both, which means a generation of people were given whichever label was noticed first and sent away with half an explanation.

That changed. Plenty of us had already spent decades finding that advice for one condition kept failing in ways that advice for the other would have predicted.

AuDHD is the informal term for being both. It is not two conditions sitting side by side, politely taking turns. The interaction produces something with its own shape.

Key takeaways

  • AuDHD is autism and ADHD together, and the two sets of needs frequently contradict each other.
  • Advice written for either condition alone tends to fail, because following it worsens the other side.
  • The contradictions can make people appear less affected than they are, which delays identification.
  • Burnout tends to arrive harder, because both sets of demands are being managed at once.
  • Designing for the conflict directly works better than picking a side.

The contradiction

The central experience is wanting two incompatible things with equal sincerity.

One part of you needs routine, predictability, and sameness. The same meal, the same route, the same sequence, because deviation is genuinely costly. The other part is bored to the point of pain by exactly that sameness and will sabotage it for novelty.

So you build a beautiful system. It works. The autistic part relaxes into the structure, which is a real relief. Then around week three the ADHD part has had enough, the system becomes intolerable, and you abandon something that was working perfectly well for reasons you cannot defend.

This happens with food, with work, with sleep, with hobbies, with the layout of a room. It is the defining pattern.

Social life runs on the same fault line. You want contact and you want to be left alone, often within the same hour. Socialising is draining in the autistic way and sought out in the ADHD way, which produces a cycle of overcommitting, enjoying it, and then needing to vanish for a fortnight.

Planning is similar. A real need to know what is happening next, and a complete inability to reliably do the thing that was planned.

The usual advice fails because it was written for one half. Follow it faithfully and you make the other half worse.

Why it goes unnoticed

The two sides partially cancel out, which makes AuDHD people look more functional than they are.

The ADHD chaos is dampened by autistic systems and rule-following, so you are not as visibly disorganised as ADHD alone would predict. The autistic rigidity is softened by ADHD flexibility and distractibility, so you are not as visibly rigid as autism alone would predict. From outside, neither picture is complete, so neither gets investigated.

What shows up instead is cost. Anxiety, depression, exhaustion, a sense of working extraordinarily hard for ordinary results. Those get treated, sometimes for years, with mixed results, because the treatment is aimed at the smoke.

The other reason is sequencing. Many people get one diagnosis, work hard on it, and find that a stubborn remainder does not respond. That remainder is frequently the other condition. If you have been told you have ADHD and the ADHD strategies only partly work, or you have been identified as autistic and your life is still chaotic in ways autism does not explain, it is worth considering.

What actually helps

The approach that works is designing for the conflict rather than pretending one side will eventually win.

Build structures with variety inside them. Fixed frame, flexible contents. A set meal time with a rotating set of options. A fixed working block where what you work on varies. This satisfies the need for predictability without triggering the boredom response, and it is the single most useful principle I have found.

Keep several systems in rotation rather than searching for the perfect one. Expect to abandon the current method every few weeks, and plan for the abandonment instead of treating it as failure. Two or three systems you cycle between will outlast one system you believe in.

Protect the non-negotiable sensory things absolutely, and let the novelty happen elsewhere. Sensory overload does not become more tolerable because the ADHD side wants excitement. Keep the environment stable and let the stimulation come from ideas, projects or people.

Make rest more specific than the word suggests. Autistic recovery usually means low sensory input and no demands. ADHD recovery usually means stimulation and novelty. Doing one when you need the other leaves you unrested, and most people have to learn to tell the difference deliberately, because both register vaguely as tiredness.

Shrink transitions wherever you can. Switching tasks is costly for the autistic side and the ADHD side both, for different reasons, which makes it unusually expensive for AuDHD people. Fewer, longer blocks beat a day full of context switches.

About burnout

It is worth saying plainly that AuDHD burnout tends to be severe.

You are running both sets of demands at once. The masking, the sensory management, the social translation, plus the constant executive effort of initiating, remembering, and finishing. Each is tiring. Together they can consume everything without producing much that looks like output, which makes the exhaustion hard to justify to yourself or anyone else.

Recovery is slower than people expect, and it is frequently interrupted. The ADHD side gets bored of resting and starts a project, which delays the recovery the autistic side needed, which deepens the burnout. If you have never managed to rest properly through a burnout, that mechanism may be why.

Common questions

Can you have both autism and ADHD?

Yes, and it is common. Diagnostic rules in several countries previously prohibited recording both, which is why many adults were given only one. Those rules have changed, though clinical practice has not caught up everywhere, and some assessors still look for one at a time.

Is AuDHD an official diagnosis?

No. It is a community term for having both autism and ADHD. You would be diagnosed with each separately, and no clinician will write AuDHD on a form. The word exists because the combination produces experiences that neither diagnosis describes on its own.

How do I know if I am AuDHD rather than just one?

Usually through the contradictions. Needing routine and destroying it. Wanting people and needing to disappear. Being organised about some things in a way that looks obsessive and chaotic about others in a way that looks careless. If strategies for one condition keep making a different part of your life worse, that is the pattern worth looking at.

Do the traits cancel each other out?

They mask each other from the outside, which is different from cancelling out internally. The effort of managing both continues regardless of how it appears. This is a large part of why AuDHD people are often identified late and are frequently told they seem fine.

Where should I start?

With the conflict itself rather than with either condition separately. Work out which of your needs are sensory and non-negotiable, and which are about stimulation and can be moved.
