Glossary

ADHD and autism terms, in plain words.

38 terms you will meet in ADHD and autistic communities, from masking and stimming to time blindness and the ADHD tax. Short definitions, written from lived experience rather than clinical manuals, with links to the longer articles.

ABDEFHILMNPRSTU

A

ADHD

Also called: attention deficit hyperactivity disorder

Attention deficit hyperactivity disorder, a lifelong neurodevelopmental condition that affects attention, impulse control, activity levels and the executive functions that organise behaviour. In adults it often shows up less as visible hyperactivity and more as difficulty starting tasks, managing time, regulating emotion and following through.

Read more: Diagnosed with ADHD as an adult: what the first year is actually like · The Late-Diagnosed ADHD Workbook

The ADHD tax

A community term for the money, and sometimes time, lost to ADHD-related difficulties: late fees on bills you could afford, forgotten subscriptions, replacing things you already own, and paying extra for convenience when the cheaper option had too many steps. It is not an official financial or clinical term.

Read more: The ADHD tax: what it actually costs, and why budgeting advice keeps missing it · The ADHD Tax

Alexithymia

Difficulty identifying and describing your own emotions. It is a trait rather than a diagnosis, and it occurs more often in autistic people than in the general population. Someone with alexithymia may notice the physical side of a feeling, like a tight chest, without being able to name the emotion behind it.

Read more: Autistic meltdown vs shutdown: what is happening, and what helps

Analysis paralysis

Also called: decision paralysis

Being unable to make a decision because you keep comparing options instead of choosing one. With ADHD it often hits small decisions hardest, because they have close options, no deadline and no obvious criterion, so the comparison never has to end.

Read more: Decision paralysis: why small choices cost the most · The ADHD Decision Reset

ARFID

Also called: avoidant/restrictive food intake disorder

Avoidant/restrictive food intake disorder, an eating disorder in which intake is limited by sensory sensitivity, fear of consequences such as choking, or low interest in food, rather than by concern about weight or body shape. It occurs at higher rates in autistic people and people with ADHD, and needs professional assessment rather than self-management.

Read more: Meal paralysis: why you forget to eat, then cannot decide what to eat

AuDHD

An informal term for someone who is both autistic and ADHD. It is not a separate diagnosis, since each condition is diagnosed on its own. The word exists because the combination produces conflicts, such as needing routine while craving novelty, that neither diagnosis describes alone.

Read more: AuDHD: what it is like being autistic and ADHD at the same time

Autism

Also called: autism spectrum disorder, ASD

A lifelong neurodevelopmental difference affecting social communication, sensory processing, and a preference for predictability and focused interests. Clinically it is called autism spectrum disorder. Many autistic adults prefer the word autistic. Adults who mask are frequently identified late.

Read more: Finding out you are autistic in adulthood · The Late-Diagnosed Autistic Workbook

Autistic burnout

A period of long-term exhaustion, loss of previously held skills, and reduced tolerance for sensory input, usually caused by sustained masking and demands that exceed capacity over months or years. It is widely described by autistic adults but is not a formal diagnosis. It differs from depression, although the two can occur together.

Read more: Autistic burnout, ADHD burnout and depression: telling them apart

B

Body doubling

Doing a task while another person is present, in the room or on a video call, without them helping. Many people with ADHD find it much easier to start and keep going this way, particularly with boring or avoided tasks.

Read more: Body doubling: why ADHD tasks get easier when someone else is in the room

D

Doom pile

Also called: doom box, doom piling

A pile or container of mixed items swept together to clear a surface quickly, meant to be sorted later. The acronym is usually given as "didn't organise, only moved". It forms because putting items away one by one requires a decision for every object, and it tends to persist because the result is an open-ended task.

Read more: Doom piles and doom boxes: the ADHD clutter cycle

Double empathy problem

The idea that communication difficulties between autistic and non-autistic people run in both directions, rather than coming from an autistic deficit alone. Each group can find the other hard to read, and autistic people often communicate easily with one another.

Read more: Finding out you are autistic in adulthood

E

Echolalia

Repeating words or phrases heard elsewhere, either immediately or later. It is common in autism and can serve communication, processing or self-regulation. In adults it often takes the form of quotes or scripts borrowed from films, shows or other people.

Emotional dysregulation

Difficulty managing the intensity and duration of emotional responses. It is not among the formal diagnostic criteria for ADHD, but it is very commonly reported by adults with ADHD and is a large part of how many of them experience the condition.

Read more: Rejection sensitive dysphoria: when criticism feels physical

Executive dysfunction

Also called: executive function

Difficulty with the executive functions: the mental processes used to plan, start, organise, switch between and finish tasks, hold information in mind, and manage time. It is central to ADHD and common in autism. It often feels like knowing exactly what to do and being unable to begin.

Read more: ADHD task paralysis: when you cannot start, even though you want to · Time blindness: why ADHD makes you late, and what actually helps

F

Functioning labels

Also called: high functioning, low functioning

Informal terms describing how well an autistic person appears to be managing from the outside. They are not diagnostic categories, and most autistic adults reject them: "high functioning" is often used to deny support to people coping at hidden cost, and "low functioning" is read as an absence of ability.

Read more: Levels of autism, and why 'high functioning' is falling out of use

H

Hyperfocus

Intense absorption in a task that is hard to break, often with lost awareness of time, hunger or tiredness. It is not under voluntary control: it attaches to whatever is interesting or urgent enough, which is not always the thing that needs doing.

Read more: Hyperfocus without the crash: using the good days without paying for them twice · The ADHD Side Hustle

I

Inattentive ADHD

Also called: ADD, inattentive presentation

The presentation of ADHD involving attention, organisation and follow-through without prominent outward hyperactivity. Restlessness is often internal rather than visible. It is frequently missed in childhood because it disrupts nobody else, and it is the same condition as other presentations.

Read more: Inattentive ADHD in adults: the quiet presentation · ADHD in women: why it is missed, and how it actually shows up

Interoception

The sense of your body's internal state, including hunger, thirst, temperature, needing the toilet, and the physical side of emotions. Differences in interoception are common in autism and ADHD, and they help explain forgetting to eat or only noticing overwhelm once it is already severe.

Read more: Meal paralysis: why you forget to eat, then cannot decide what to eat

L

Late diagnosis

Also called: late identification

Being identified as ADHD or autistic in adulthood rather than childhood. It is common among people who masked well, did well at school, or did not match the narrow descriptions used when they were young. It often brings relief and grief at the same time.

Read more: Diagnosed with ADHD as an adult: what the first year is actually like · Finding out you are autistic in adulthood

M

Masking

Also called: camouflaging

Suppressing natural behaviour and performing expected behaviour to fit in: forcing eye contact, scripting conversations, hiding stimming, copying how other people act. It is common in autistic people and also occurs in ADHD. It is tiring to sustain and is strongly associated with burnout.

Read more: Masking, and what it costs · The Late-Diagnosed Autistic Workbook

Meal paralysis

Also called: food paralysis

Being hungry and wanting to eat but being unable to choose food or start preparing it. It is a community term for executive dysfunction applied to eating, and it is often worse when hunger has already become urgent.

Read more: Meal paralysis: why you forget to eat, then cannot decide what to eat · The ADHD Eating Reset

Meltdown

An involuntary reaction to overwhelming stress or sensory input, in which a person loses control of their responses. It may involve crying, shouting or physical agitation. It is not a tantrum, because it is neither chosen nor aimed at getting something.

Read more: Autistic meltdown vs shutdown: what is happening, and what helps

Monotropism

A theory of autism proposing that autistic attention tends to concentrate deeply on a small number of interests at once, instead of spreading across many. It is used to explain both the depth of autistic focus and the difficulty of switching between tasks.

Read more: AuDHD: what it is like being autistic and ADHD at the same time

N

Neurodiversity

Also called: neurodivergent

The idea that variation in how brains work, including autism and ADHD, is a natural part of human diversity. A neurodivergent person is someone whose brain works differently from the typical pattern.

P

Panic attack

A sudden episode of intense fear with strong physical symptoms such as a racing heart, shortness of breath, chest tightness and dizziness, often with a sense of dread. It differs from an autistic meltdown mainly in being driven by fear rather than accumulated overload, and the two can occur together.

Read more: Autistic meltdown or panic attack? Telling them apart

PDA

Also called: pathological demand avoidance, pervasive drive for autonomy, demand avoidance

Extreme avoidance of everyday demands, understood as driven by anxiety rather than defiance, and applying to demands you set yourself as well as those from other people. It is not a formal diagnosis and does not appear separately in the main diagnostic manuals; where it is used, it is usually described as a profile within autism.

Read more: PDA: demand avoidance in autistic adults

R

Rejection sensitive dysphoria

Also called: RSD, rejection sensitivity

Intense emotional pain in response to real or perceived rejection, criticism or failure. The term is widely used in ADHD communities to describe something many people with ADHD recognise immediately. It is not a formal diagnosis and does not appear in diagnostic manuals.

Read more: Rejection sensitive dysphoria: when criticism feels physical · The ADHD Overthinking Reset

Revenge bedtime procrastination

Staying up late, even when tired, to reclaim personal time that the day did not allow. It is common among people with ADHD and anyone whose days are full of other people's demands.

Read more: The 2 a.m. spiral: why ADHD overthinking happens at night

Right to Choose

In England, the right to choose which provider carries out your first NHS appointment for a new problem, rather than being referred automatically to the local service. Commonly used to shorten long waits for ADHD and autism assessment. It does not apply in Scotland, Wales or Northern Ireland.

Read more: NHS Right to Choose for ADHD and autism assessment

Rumination

Repetitive, circular thinking about the same worry or event without reaching a conclusion. It feels like problem-solving but produces no decisions, and it is often worst at night when there is nothing else to occupy attention.

Read more: The 2 a.m. spiral: why ADHD overthinking happens at night · The ADHD Overthinking Reset

S

Sensory overload

When the amount or intensity of sensory input, such as noise, light, touch or smell, exceeds what the brain can process. It can cause distress, irritability, shutdown or meltdown. It is very common in autism and also occurs in ADHD.

Read more: Stimming and sensory overload in autistic adults · Autistic meltdown vs shutdown: what is happening, and what helps

Shutdown

A response to overwhelm in which a person withdraws and becomes less responsive, sometimes temporarily losing the ability to speak or move easily. Like a meltdown it is involuntary, but the reaction turns inward rather than outward.

Read more: Autistic meltdown vs shutdown: what is happening, and what helps

Special interest

An intense, long-lasting interest that an autistic person finds deeply absorbing. Many autistic adults describe their special interests as a source of joy and regulation rather than simply a hobby.

Spoon theory

A metaphor in which daily energy is a limited number of spoons, and every task, including small ones, costs some. It began in the chronic illness community and is now widely used by disabled and neurodivergent people to explain why capacity runs out.

Read more: Autistic burnout, ADHD burnout and depression: telling them apart

Stimming

Also called: self-stimulatory behaviour

Repetitive movements or sounds, such as rocking, hand-flapping, tapping, humming or fidgeting, used to self-regulate. It is common in autism and also occurs in ADHD. Many autistic adults were trained to suppress it as children.

Read more: Stimming and sensory overload in autistic adults

T

Task paralysis

Also called: ADHD paralysis

Being unable to start a task despite wanting or needing to, sometimes to the point where beginning feels physically impossible. It is linked to executive dysfunction and is one of the most commonly described experiences of adult ADHD.

Read more: ADHD task paralysis: when you cannot start, even though you want to · The ADHD Decision Reset

Time blindness

Difficulty sensing the passage of time and estimating how long things will take, commonly reported by people with ADHD. It leads to lateness, missed deadlines and tasks that take far longer than expected. It is a community term rather than a clinical one.

Read more: Time blindness: why ADHD makes you late, and what actually helps · The Late-Diagnosed ADHD Workbook

U

Unmasking

Gradually reducing masking, usually starting in private and extending selectively to safe people and places. It tends to be slow and partial, and safety has to come first.

Read more: Masking, and what it costs

I write from lived experience as an autistic adult with ADHD, not as a clinician. Nothing here diagnoses anything or replaces working with a doctor, therapist, or ADHD coach. If something in your life has become unmanageable, please talk to someone qualified.
