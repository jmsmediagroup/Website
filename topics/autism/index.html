Autism in adults

Writing on autistic adult life from the inside: masking, stimming and sensory overload, meltdowns and shutdowns, burnout, work, and late identification.

Most writing about autism describes it from the outside, in terms of what other people can observe. This collection tries to describe it from the inside: what masking costs, why a noisy office can be harder than the work itself, what a shutdown feels like, and why a day that looked easy can take three days to recover from.

Many of the adults these articles are written for were identified late, often after decades of camouflaging so effectively that even they did not see it. For them, the useful questions are rarely about deficits. They are about cost and fit: where the effort is going, which environments drain it fastest, and what changes make daily life less expensive to run.

If you are new to this, start with finding out you are autistic in adulthood and masking, and what it costs. The autistic-led organisations listed below are, in my experience, the best next step after anything written here.

Key terms

Autism
A lifelong neurodevelopmental difference affecting social communication, sensory processing, and a preference for predictability and focused interests.
Masking
Suppressing natural behaviour and performing expected behaviour to fit in: forcing eye contact, scripting conversations, hiding stimming, copying how other people act.
Unmasking
Gradually reducing masking, usually starting in private and extending selectively to safe people and places.
Stimming
Repetitive movements or sounds, such as rocking, hand-flapping, tapping, humming or fidgeting, used to self-regulate.
Sensory overload
When the amount or intensity of sensory input, such as noise, light, touch or smell, exceeds what the brain can process.
Meltdown
An involuntary reaction to overwhelming stress or sensory input, in which a person loses control of their responses.
Shutdown
A response to overwhelm in which a person withdraws and becomes less responsive, sometimes temporarily losing the ability to speak or move easily.
Autistic burnout
A period of long-term exhaustion, loss of previously held skills, and reduced tolerance for sensory input, usually caused by sustained masking and demands that exceed capacity over months or years.
Monotropism
A theory of autism proposing that autistic attention tends to concentrate deeply on a small number of interests at once, instead of spreading across many.
Special interest
An intense, long-lasting interest that an autistic person finds deeply absorbing.
Double empathy problem
The idea that communication difficulties between autistic and non-autistic people run in both directions, rather than coming from an autistic deficit alone.
Alexithymia
Difficulty identifying and describing your own emotions.

Organisations and help

Autism organisations

Several of these are run by autistic people themselves, which tends to show in how they write about autism: as a difference to understand and accommodate rather than a problem to fix.

  • Autistic Self Advocacy Network (ASAN)United States

    A nonprofit run by and for autistic people, working on policy and self-advocacy, with resources on health care, education, community living and accessibility.

  • Autistic Women & Nonbinary Network (AWN)United States

    A nonprofit focused on autistic women, girls, nonbinary and trans people, working from a neurodiversity and disability justice perspective.

  • National Autistic SocietyUnited Kingdom

    The UK's main autism charity, with advice across fourteen topic areas from diagnosis and employment to money and relationships, an online community, and an autism services directory.

  • Reframing AutismAustralia

    An autistic-led charity with a free Autistic Welcome Pack and a free Autism Essentials course, plus resources for people who are newly identified or still questioning.

  • AutisticaUnited Kingdom

    A UK autism research and campaigning charity focused on helping autistic people live long, healthy lives.

Getting assessed as an adult

Official information on how adult assessment works. Routes, waits and costs differ a great deal between countries, so start with the source for where you live.

  • NHS: ADHD in adultsUnited Kingdom

    How adult ADHD shows up and how assessment works on the NHS, starting with an appointment with a GP.

  • NHS: Autism assessmentsUnited Kingdom

    What an NHS autism assessment involves and how to ask for one as an adult.

  • ADHD UK: Right to ChooseEngland

    In England you have a legal right to choose who carries out your NHS ADHD or autism assessment, which can shorten long local waits. This guide explains how to ask your GP.

  • NICE guideline NG87: ADHDUnited Kingdom

    The clinical guideline UK services follow for diagnosing and managing ADHD, including in adults.

  • NICE guideline CG142: Autism in adultsUnited Kingdom

    The clinical guideline for recognising, referring and diagnosing autism in adults in the UK.

  • NIMH: ADHDUnited States

    Plain-language information on ADHD from the US National Institute of Mental Health.

  • NIMH: Autism spectrum disorderUnited States

    Plain-language information on autism from the US National Institute of Mental Health.

Work and reasonable adjustments

Your rights at work depend on where you live and who employs you. These are the official and expert sources, and they are a better guide than anything general I could write.

  • Job Accommodation Network (JAN)United States

    Free, expert and confidential guidance on workplace accommodations, for employees and employers.

  • JAN: ADHD accommodation ideasUnited States

    Practical workplace accommodation ideas for ADHD, organised by the difficulty they address.

  • JAN: Autism accommodation ideasUnited States

    Practical workplace accommodation ideas for autistic employees.

  • EEOC: Disability-related resourcesUnited States

    The US federal agency that enforces disability discrimination law at work. Its employment protections apply to employers with 15 or more employees.

  • Acas: Reasonable adjustments at workUnited Kingdom

    What reasonable adjustments are and when the Equality Act 2010 requires an employer to make them.

  • Access to WorkEngland, Scotland and Wales

    A UK government scheme that can fund support at work for disabled people and people with health conditions. Northern Ireland has a separate system.
