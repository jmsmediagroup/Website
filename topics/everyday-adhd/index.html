Everyday ADHD: focus, time and feelings

Articles on how ADHD shows up day to day: task paralysis, time blindness, overthinking, rejection sensitivity, decisions, food, work and relationships.

Most of ADHD in adult life is not dramatic. It is the email you have meant to send for nine days, the meeting you were late for despite thinking about it all morning, the menu you could not choose from, the comment from a colleague that you replayed until two in the morning.

These are the articles about that everyday layer: starting things, feeling time pass, making decisions, eating, sleeping, working, and the emotional intensity that sits underneath much of it. Each one tries to explain what is actually going on before suggesting anything, because most of the standard advice fails by misreading the problem. Trying harder does not fix a task you cannot start, and a better planner does not fix a sense of time that splits into now and not now.

A good place to begin is task paralysis, since being unable to start sits behind so much else, followed by time blindness and rejection sensitive dysphoria.

Key terms

Task paralysis
Being unable to start a task despite wanting or needing to, sometimes to the point where beginning feels physically impossible.
Time blindness
Difficulty sensing the passage of time and estimating how long things will take, commonly reported by people with ADHD.
Rejection sensitive dysphoria
Intense emotional pain in response to real or perceived rejection, criticism or failure.
Body doubling
Doing a task while another person is present, in the room or on a video call, without them helping.
Rumination
Repetitive, circular thinking about the same worry or event without reaching a conclusion.
Analysis paralysis
Being unable to make a decision because you keep comparing options instead of choosing one.
Meal paralysis
Being hungry and wanting to eat but being unable to choose food or start preparing it.
Emotional dysregulation
Difficulty managing the intensity and duration of emotional responses.

Organisations and help

ADHD organisations

National charities and nonprofits for adults with ADHD. Most offer free information, and several run peer support groups, helplines or directories of professionals.

  • ADDA, Attention Deficit Disorder AssociationInternational

    An organisation focused specifically on adults with ADHD, offering virtual support groups, webinars, a professional directory, and body doubling sessions for members.

  • CHADDUnited States

    A US nonprofit that runs the National Resource Center on ADHD, with fact sheets, local chapters, online communities, and directories of ADHD professionals and centres.

  • ADHD UKUnited Kingdom

    A UK charity with information on getting diagnosed, including the NHS Right to Choose route, plus guidance on work and education and online support groups.

  • CADDAC, Centre for ADHD Awareness CanadaCanada

    A national charity working on ADHD awareness, education and advocacy across Canada.

  • ADHD AustraliaAustralia

    A national organisation providing ADHD information, resources and community support for individuals, families, educators and employers.

Peer support and body doubling

Talking to people who already understand saves a great deal of explaining. Virtual body doubling is included here because, for a lot of ADHD adults, other people's presence is what makes starting possible.

  • Adult ADHD community (CHADD on HealthUnlocked)International

    A free online peer community for adults with ADHD, run with CHADD.

  • Women with ADHD community (CHADD on HealthUnlocked)International

    A free online peer community for women with ADHD, run with CHADD.

  • FocusmateInternational

    Virtual body doubling: short video sessions where you and another person each work on your own task. There is a free tier.

Related: Body doubling: why ADHD tasks get easier when someone else is in the room

Work and reasonable adjustments

Your rights at work depend on where you live and who employs you. These are the official and expert sources, and they are a better guide than anything general I could write.

  • Job Accommodation Network (JAN)United States

    Free, expert and confidential guidance on workplace accommodations, for employees and employers.

  • JAN: ADHD accommodation ideasUnited States

    Practical workplace accommodation ideas for ADHD, organised by the difficulty they address.

  • JAN: Autism accommodation ideasUnited States

    Practical workplace accommodation ideas for autistic employees.

  • EEOC: Disability-related resourcesUnited States

    The US federal agency that enforces disability discrimination law at work. Its employment protections apply to employers with 15 or more employees.

  • Acas: Reasonable adjustments at workUnited Kingdom

    What reasonable adjustments are and when the Equality Act 2010 requires an employer to make them.

  • Access to WorkEngland, Scotland and Wales

    A UK government scheme that can fund support at work for disabled people and people with health conditions. Northern Ireland has a separate system.
